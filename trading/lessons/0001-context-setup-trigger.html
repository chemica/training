Lesson 0001 · 30 minutes · Price action foundations

Turn a chart story into a testable trade hypothesis.

Today’s win: analyse one crypto market through three timeframes—without letting a dramatic small candle overrule the larger auction.

0–3 min · Retrieve before reading

Without opening a chart, write answers to three questions:

  1. What information disappears when you move from a 5-minute chart to a 4-hour chart?
  2. What becomes easier to see?
  3. What makes a market opinion falsifiable?
Reveal after answering

Aggregation hides the path within each four-hour interval: trade sequence, short-lived levels, spread and intrabar volatility. It makes broader swings, regime and structural location easier to distinguish. An opinion is falsifiable when it names observable evidence that would show it is wrong. Neither scale is “truer”; each answers a different question.

3–8 min · Give intuition precise language

Experienced chart-watchers often see something useful but describe it with elastic words—“bullish”, “strong”, “looks supported”. Replace those with a small, stable vocabulary:

Timeframe
The fixed interval compressed into one candle. A 15-minute candle records that interval’s open, high, low and close; it does not reveal the exact path between them.
Context
The higher-timeframe environment: regime, volatility and current location relative to visible structure. It changes expectations; it does not predict the next candle.
Regime
A working classification: trend, range or transition. A trend makes directional progress with pullbacks; a range repeatedly rejects boundaries; transition fits neither reliably yet.
Structure
Visible swing highs/lows, range boundaries and areas where behaviour previously changed. Treat them as zones, not laser-precise lines.
Setup
A named behaviour at a relevant location that could create opportunity—for example, a pullback holding above a prior breakout zone. It is not permission to enter.
Trigger
A predeclared, observable execution-timeframe event permitting a simulated entry. “Looks good” is not a trigger; “15m closes above the zone, then exceeds that candle’s high” is.
Invalidation
Market evidence incompatible with the hypothesis. It is why the idea is abandoned, not merely where a stop happens to sit.
Acceptance / rejection
Descriptive shorthand. Acceptance means price spends time and trades repeatedly beyond a zone; rejection means it probes beyond and returns. Define your evidence—there is no universal candle count.

Candles are summaries, not psychological X-rays. A crypto chart is built from venue-specific trades; even Coinbase documents candles and market trades as distinct feeds. This helps explain why exchanges can print slightly different wicks. See the Coinbase market-data specification.

8–15 min · One hierarchy, three jobs

Context · 4h

What auction am I entering?

Classify regime and location. Mark at most two zones. Note volatility expansion or contraction.

Setup · 1h

What behaviour matters here?

Specify pullback, breakout, failed breakout or mean reversion—and where it counts.

Trigger · 15m/5m

What permits action?

Wait for an observable condition. This scale times execution; it does not rewrite context.

The hierarchy is not “higher timeframe always wins”. Each timeframe has one job. A long trigger inside a 4h downtrend may be coherent if explicitly countertrend with adjusted expectations. The error is discovering the conflict after entry, then switching timeframes to defend the position.

Read candles relationally

Do not merely name the final candle. Ask: Where is it? What preceded it? Did it close beyond meaningful structure? What later evidence confirms or rejects the interpretation? “Hammer” and “engulfing” describe shape; they do not supply location, cause or edge.

15–20 min · Worked example

Fictional BTC/USD replay · no recommendation

Observed: On 4h, price rotated between 61,800–62,200 and 65,600–66,100 three times. It is near the upper zone. On 1h, price briefly traded to 66,350 but closed below 66,100. On 15m, it compresses below 66,100.

Weak story: “That wick is bearish; short BTC.” It confuses one candle with a forecast and cannot be cleanly disproved.

  1. Context: 4h range; price tests the 65,600–66,100 upper boundary.
  2. Setup: possible failed upside breakout, only if price remains inside the range.
  3. Trigger: after an hourly close below 65,600, a 15m retest of the zone fails and closes below the retest candle’s low.
  4. Invalidation: hourly close above 66,350 followed by continued trading above 66,100—evidence of acceptance.

Why better: another person can determine whether each condition happened. It separates observation from inference, waits for evidence and can resolve as “no trade”. Profit would not prove the reasoning; loss would not automatically disprove it.

Support and resistance are areas rather than guaranteed exact prices; read CME Group’s primer.

20–28 min · Paper-trading lab

Open historical replay for one liquid spot pair, hide indicators and conceal future candles. Spend eight minutes:

  1. 2 min, 4h: classify trend/range/transition; mark at most two zones.
  2. 2 min, 1h: name one location-dependent setup—or “no setup”.
  3. 2 min, 15m: write a trigger before advancing replay.
  4. 2 min: write invalidation and capture screenshot A; advance 10–20 candles and capture B.

28–30 min · Retrieval test

1. A 4h range contains a 5m upside break. Best first conclusion?
2. Which is genuine invalidation?
3. Why is “bullish engulfing candle” incomplete?
Answer key and explanations
  1. B. The break is evidence, not proof of a 4h change.
  2. C. It specifies market evidence incompatible with the range-failure thesis.
  3. A. Shape without context is a description, not a setup; B and C are unsupported absolutes.

Close the feedback loop

  1. Save both screenshots and the four-line hypothesis.
  2. Explain which conditions occurred; do not lead with hypothetical profit.
  3. Ask your LLM teacher: “Was my hypothesis observable, internally consistent and falsifiable? Separate hindsight from information available at screenshot A.”
