Lesson 0008 · 30 minutes · Playbook

A breach is an event. Acceptance is behaviour.

Today’s win: turn a dramatic move through a boundary into two testable hypotheses—continuation or failure—without letting one wick make the decision.

Paper-only rule. Complete this lesson in replay or a paper account. Crypto trades continuously and leveraged positions can be liquidated quickly. The objective is classification and execution practice, not profit.

0–4 · Retrieve before reading

Without looking anything up, write three observations after price crosses resistance that would support acceptance above it. Make each visible on a chart.

4–11 · Six terms, one sequence

Breach
Price trades beyond a pre-declared boundary. A wick one tick above resistance is a breach; it says where price traded, not whether the market accepted that price.
Acceptance
Repeated behaviour consistent with trading on the new side: closes beyond the boundary, time transacting there, pullbacks holding it, and structure developing away. No single item is mandatory; evidence accumulates.
Rejection
Price probes beyond the boundary but promptly returns through it. A long wick is evidence; the following bars must show whether price can re-establish itself outside.
Failed breakout
A breach that first meets your written continuation trigger, then violates its invalidation and returns to prior structure. Without a prewritten trigger, “failed breakout” is often hindsight.
Retest
A return toward the breached boundary after price has moved away. A retest is an event, not automatically an entry: it may hold, cut through, or churn around the level.
Trap
A trader-centred description for a move that induces entries on one side before reversing and forcing exits. You can observe the reversal; you usually cannot prove who was trapped or why.

11–17 · Competing hypotheses

H₁ · Acceptance

Claim: the market is building value beyond the old range.

Evidence: closes outside; shallow pullback; boundary holds; new swing structure forms outside; room exists before the next higher-timeframe obstacle.

Wrong if: decisive return into the range or no continuation within your stated time.

H₂ · Rejection/failure

Claim: prices beyond the boundary were not sustained.

Evidence: quick return inside; attempted reclaim fails; lower high forms below resistance; travel toward the range interior.

Wrong if: price reclaims and holds the boundary, building structure outside again.

H₀ · No trade

Claim: evidence is mixed or reward is poor.

Evidence: repeated crossings; wide bars make invalidation expensive; nearby obstacle; thin liquidity; event-driven volatility.

Action: wait. Undecided is a valid classification.

Write H₁ and H₂ before revealing bars. A trigger names an observable event; an invalidation states what would show the premise is wrong. Invalidation of H₁ does not automatically activate H₂.

Common failure modes

  • Boundary shopping: redrawing the level after the outcome. Freeze the zone before the breach.
  • Wick worship: calling every probe a fakeout. Read the close and following response in context.
  • Late confirmation: waiting until most of the move is gone, then accepting poor reward-to-risk. No trade beats forced certainty.
  • Lower-timeframe tunnel vision: buying into higher-timeframe resistance or shorting into support. Mark the next obstacle first.
  • Volume certainty: high volume shows activity, not participants’ intent; the price response matters.
  • Ignoring friction: spread, fees and slippage can erase a paper edge, especially in crypto scalping.

17–21 · Worked example

On a paper BTC/USD 15-minute chart, suppose a four-hour range has resistance at £49,900–£50,000. The next higher-timeframe obstacle is £50,600.

H₁ trigger: a 15-minute close above £50,000, followed within three bars by a pullback no lower than £49,850 and then a close above £50,050.

H₁ invalidation: a 15-minute close below £49,850 after activation. A hypothetical paper entry at £50,080 with invalidation at £49,840 risks £240 per BTC. A £50,560 target offers £480: 2R before costs.

H₂ trigger: after the breach, a close below £49,850, then an attempted rally that fails to close above £50,000.

H₂ invalidation: a close above £50,100. Its first objective is the range midpoint—not automatically the opposite edge.

R = |entry − invalidation| × position size
(£50,560 − £50,080) ÷ (£50,080 − £49,840) = 2R before costs

If price chops across £50,000 without either sequence, neither branch activates. “Buy because it looks strong” is not testable.

21–27 · Timed forked replay

  1. Minute 0–1: Open a liquid crypto pair in replay; use 15-minute execution and 1-hour context; hide future bars.
  2. Minute 1–2: Mark a prior range boundary and next obstacle. Do not move them.
  3. Minute 2–3: Write H₁ and H₂ triggers, invalidations, and a no-trade condition.
  4. Minute 3–5: Reveal one bar at a time. Label each state: breach, acceptance evidence, rejection evidence, activated, invalidated, or undecided.
  5. Minute 5–6: Stop after activation plus five bars, or ten bars without activation. Screenshot setup and outcome.

Replay record

27–30 · Test your discrimination

1. Price wicks above resistance and closes inside. Is this a failed breakout?

2. Price closes above resistance on high volume, then crosses the boundary both ways for four bars. Which state is best?

3. H₁ activates, price returns inside, then immediately reclaims and holds above. Must you reverse short?

Self-score (open after all three)

3/3: move to replay. 2/3: replay, but narrate each state aloud. 0–1/3: redraw the decision map from memory tomorrow before practice.

Feedback artefact

Send your two screenshots and branches to your LLM teacher with:

Audit this breakout replay without hindsight. Could two traders apply each trigger and invalidation consistently? Identify boundary shopping, future leakage, ambiguous time limits, ignored obstacles, or missing costs. Do not suggest a live trade.
