Lesson 0005 · 30 minutes · Paper only

Price is visible. Execution is the bill.

Today’s win: read the market as a place where orders meet—not merely a line on a chart—and reject trades whose edge disappears after realistic costs.

Safety boundary. Every order in this chapter is hypothetical. Observe a live market, but do not place an order. Crypto markets are volatile, fragmented, and may lack protections found in regulated securities markets. Never treat an LLM’s output as a signal or as a substitute for venue documentation.

0–4 · Retrieve before reading

Answer from memory: can a market show high recent volume yet be expensive to enter immediately? Name two ways this can happen.

Check your answer

Yes. Volume records completed activity over an interval; it does not promise executable size now. A fast market may print high volume while its spread widens, nearby orders are cancelled, depth is thin, or price moves between the decision and the fill.

4–12 · The vocabulary of execution

Volume

The quantity already traded during a stated interval. “2,000 BTC in one hour” is meaningless without the instrument, venue, and interval.

Liquidity

The ability to trade the desired size, promptly, with little price concession. It is conditional on size, side, venue, and time.

Spread

The gap between the highest displayed bid and lowest displayed ask. In basis points: (ask − bid) ÷ midpoint × 10,000.

Depth

The resting quantity available at successive prices in the order book. Top-of-book size is only the first level, not total depth.

Slippage

The difference between a reference price when you decided or submitted and the average realised fill. Define the reference before measuring it.

Market impact

The component of price movement caused by your own order consuming liquidity or signalling demand. Slippage can also come from latency and other traders.

Maker, taker, and the limit-order trap

A maker order rests on the book and adds displayed liquidity. A taker order executes immediately against resting orders and removes liquidity. Venues commonly price these differently. A marketable limit order can still be a taker; “limit” does not automatically mean “maker.” A post-only instruction, where supported, rejects or reprices an order that would immediately cross.

Lower maker fees are not free alpha. A resting order may never fill, may fill only partially, or may fill just before price moves against it—adverse selection. Execution quality includes missed opportunity and post-fill movement, not fees alone.

Fragmentation

Crypto trading is split across exchanges, jurisdictions, spot pairs, perpetual futures, and quote currencies. Each venue has its own book, fees, uptime, customers, and rules. “BTC volume” is therefore not one consolidated fact. A deep BTC-USD book on one exchange does not guarantee depth on BTC-GBP elsewhere; apparent arbitrage may be offset by transfer time, withdrawal limits, funding, basis, fees, or counterparty risk.

12–18 · Read a book, not a screenshot

Suppose BTC’s midpoint is approximately £50,000. This simplified ask side is offered:

Ask levelPriceBTC here£ valueCumulative £
Best ask£50,0000.005£250.00£250.00
Level 2£50,0100.010£500.10£750.10
Level 3£50,0300.020£1,000.60£1,750.70

A £1,000 market buy cannot all execute at £50,000. It consumes levels in price-time priority: £250 at £50,000; £500.10 at £50,010; and the remaining £249.90 at £50,030. The approximate volume-weighted average price (VWAP) is £50,012.50—about 2.5 basis points above the best ask. That shortfall is not a mysterious broker error: it is the visible consequence of insufficient depth for the requested size.

VWAP = total cash traded ÷ total units filled
spread (bps) = (best ask − best bid) ÷ midpoint × 10,000

Real books change while an order travels and executes. Displayed quantity may cancel, appear, or be hidden; this static example is a model, not a prediction.

18–22 · Worked round-trip cost audit

Assume a hypothetical £1,000 BTC spot trade, unchanged midpoint, best bid £49,990 and ask £50,010. The quoted spread is £20, or 4 bps. Assume taker fees of 0.40% on entry and exit, plus 0.02% slippage each way. These are teaching assumptions—replace them with your venue’s current schedule and observed book.

  1. Entry fee: £1,000 × 0.40% = £4.00.
  2. Exit fee: approximately £1,000 × 0.40% = £4.00.
  3. Crossing the full spread: £1,000 × 0.04% = £0.40 for an immediate buy then sell.
  4. Additional slippage: £1,000 × (0.02% + 0.02%) = £0.40.
  5. Estimated round-trip friction: £8.80, or 0.88% / 88 bps.
net P&L ≈ gross P&L − fees − spread paid − slippage − financing/funding
break-even gross move ≈ total round-trip friction = 0.88%

A forecast 0.60% move has negative expectancy under these assumptions before tax. The correct response is not automatically “use maker orders”: first ask whether the strategy can tolerate non-fill and adverse-selection risk.

22–27 · Timed observation drill

Five minutes · observe only · no order ticket

  1. Minute 1: choose one BTC spot pair and write the venue, pair, and UK time. Verify that it is spot—not a perpetual or CFD.
  2. Minute 2: record best bid, best ask, displayed size at each, and calculate spread in pounds and basis points.
  3. Minute 3: inspect enough levels to estimate the average fill for hypothetical £100, £1,000, and £10,000 market buys. Record “unavailable” if the interface does not expose depth.
  4. Minute 4: count how often the top quote changes. Note one moment when volume rises and whether spread/depth improve or deteriorate.
  5. Minute 5: use the venue’s official fee schedule to estimate all-in round-trip friction for the £1,000 case. State every assumption.

Save this locally or send it to your LLM teacher. Do not include account identifiers, API keys, balances, or screenshots containing personal information.
