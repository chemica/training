Lesson 0011 · 30 minutes · Execution

An order chooses a failure mode.

Today’s win: translate a chart idea into a paper order whose execution behaviour—and failure mode—you can explain before price moves.

Paper-only boundary. Every ticket here is a simulation. Do not place a live order. Crypto trades continuously, can gap between quotes, and venue rules differ. Verify exact behaviour, fees, and trigger sources before risking money.

0–4 · Retrieve before reading

Write two sentences from memory: When would you care more about getting filled than controlling price? When would you accept missing a trade to control price?

Calibration

A protective exit after invalidation commonly prioritises leaving; a patient pullback entry commonly prioritises price. Neither rule is universal: liquidity, size, volatility, and the cost of being wrong decide.

4–11 · The tiny market inside every chart

A candle compresses transactions. An order interacts with the live auction beneath it:

Bid
The highest currently displayed price a buyer is offering.
Ask (offer)
The lowest currently displayed price a seller is offering.
Spread
Ask minus bid. Crossing it is an immediate execution cost before fees and slippage.
Queue
Orders waiting at one price. Many venues use price then time priority; joining a price does not put you first.
Partial fill
Only some requested quantity executes. The remainder stays open, cancels, or follows its time-in-force.
Adverse selection
Your order executes disproportionately when the next move is unfavourable—often because the other side reacts faster or knows more.

Worked micro-example. BTC is quoted 99.98 bid / 100.00 ask. Buying now pays at least 100.00: the quoted spread is 0.02 before fees. If only 0.7 BTC is offered there and your paper order is for 1 BTC, the remaining 0.3 must find higher asks. The average fill can exceed 100.00; that difference is slippage.

11–18 · Six instructions, six trade-offs

Market

Execute now against available opposite-side orders. Prioritises speed and fill probability, not a maximum buy or minimum sell price.

Failure: slippage.

Limit

Buy no higher, or sell no lower, than a set price. A non-marketable limit rests; a marketable limit may execute immediately up to its bound.

Failure: no or partial fill.

Stop-market

Wait for a trigger, then release a market order. It controls activation, not execution price.

Failure: slippage after trigger.

Stop-limit

Wait for the stop, then release a limit order. The stop activates; the limit bounds execution.

Failure: triggered but unfilled.

Queue position

A resting order waits behind earlier orders at its price. Trading there does not prove your turn was reached.

Failure: assuming touch equals fill.

Adverse selection

A passive “good price” may fill because price is accelerating through it. Judge the fill by what follows.

Failure: confusing cheap with informed.

execution choice = urgency × liquidity × volatility × size × consequence of non-fill
  1. Must it execute? A market-style instruction may fit—but model slippage.
  2. Is a worst price essential? A limit fits—but plan for zero or partial execution.
  3. Should it exist only after confirmation? A stop supplies the trigger; its market or limit child determines the failure mode.
  4. What if nothing fills? “Miss the trade” is valid. “Improvise” is not a plan.

18–23 · Worked paper tickets

Ticket A · patient pullback

Observation: BTC trades at 100.20; the idea is valid only near support at 100.00.

Paper instruction: limit buy 0.10 BTC at 100.02. Cancel if a five-minute candle closes below 99.80 before fill.

Why: price matters more than participation. Outcomes include no fill, partial fill, or an adversely selected fill followed by a break. A touch at 100.02 remains “unknown fill” unless replay data proves sufficient traded volume and queue priority.

Ticket B · breakout confirmation

Observation: resistance is 101.00; the hypothesis begins only above it.

Paper instruction: stop-market buy 0.10 BTC, trigger 101.05. Cancel after 30 minutes if untriggered.

Why: confirmation matters more than exact price. It may fill at 101.12 or worse. A stop-limit at stop 101.05 / limit 101.10 caps price but might trigger, jump to 101.20, and never fill.

Check 1. A stop-limit “protects price” during a fast break. What new risk appears?

Check 2. A candle low touches your limit buy, then rallies. May you record a paper fill?

23–28 · Timed paper lab

Open a liquid BTC market in replay or pause a live chart. No real orders. Set a five-minute timer for each:

  1. Pullback: choose support and draft a limit-entry ticket.
  2. Breakout: choose resistance and draft stop-market and stop-limit versions.

Every ticket must state:

  • Side, paper quantity, type, trigger and/or limit
  • Current bid, ask, spread, and visible nearby depth
  • Expected fill, worst plausible fill, and partial-fill response
  • Structural invalidation, cancellation time, and consequence of non-fill
  • Assumed maker/taker fee—and whether it needs verification

Advance replay ten bars. Grade the decision, not P&L: Did it behave as specified? Did the failure mode fit? Was any paper fill assumption unjustified?

28–30 · Exit test

1. Quote: 50.00 / 50.04. What is the spread?

2. Which instruction bounds price but can remain unfilled?

3. Why can a passive buy fill be bad news?

4. Contrast stop-market and stop-limit during a sudden drop.

Pass: 4/4. If you miss one, explain its failure mode aloud and retake tomorrow. Send the tickets to your LLM teacher: “Check whether each order fits the hypothesis. Challenge my fill assumptions, queue reasoning, and failure mode. Do not evaluate outcome P&L.”

Primary sources

Venue behaviour is an implementation detail. Read the official documentation for the venue you simulate:

  • Coinbase Advanced Trade: Create Order.
  • Kraken: market and limit orders and stop-loss limit orders.
  • Coinbase Exchange: matching engine (price-time priority and lifecycle).

Ask follow-up questions whenever an order state, venue rule, or fill assumption is unclear. Ambiguity means pause the simulation—not guess.
