Lesson 0010 · 30 minutes · Playbook

Scalping is a cost problem first.

Today’s win: turn a plausible tiny move into a complete, testable trade—and decide whether its edge survives spread, fees, slippage and execution delay.

Paper only. No leverage. This chapter is training, not a recommendation to trade. A fast strategy can produce many emotionally convincing results before there is enough evidence to estimate its true expectancy.

0–4 · What scalping actually is

Scalping is a trading approach that tries to capture small price movements over short holding periods, often repeating a narrowly defined setup many times. “Fast” is not the edge. The edge, if one exists, is a repeatable difference between the probability-weighted price movement captured and the total cost of attempting to capture it.

A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of one percent: 1 bp = 0.01%, 10 bp = 0.10%, and 100 bp = 1%. Basis points make small moves easier to compare. On a £10,000 notional position, 1 bp is £1 before costs.

Gross result

The price movement captured before any trading costs. Buy at 100.00 and sell at 100.12: +12 bp gross.

Friction

Everything between the chart idea and realised result: explicit fees, spread paid, slippage, latency and missed fills.

Net result

What remains after friction. A +12 bp gross move with 9 bp round-trip friction is only +3 bp net.

4–9 · The vocabulary of a real edge

Hit rate
Winning trades divided by all completed trades. It says nothing about the relative size of wins and losses.
Payoff ratio
Average winning trade divided by average losing trade, using positive magnitudes. A 0.5 ratio means the average win is half the average loss.
Gross expectancy
The probability-weighted average result per trade before friction.
Net expectancy
The probability-weighted average result after all round-trip friction. This is the number that matters.
Latency
The delay between observing, deciding, submitting and executing. During that delay, price and queue position can change.
Adverse selection
The tendency to get filled when the other side has better short-term information or urgency. A resting buy may fill precisely because price is about to fall.
Gross expectancy = (hit rate × average gross win) − (loss rate × average gross loss)
Net expectancy = (hit rate × average net win) − (loss rate × average net loss)
Equivalent shortcut: net expectancy ≈ gross expectancy − average round-trip friction

The shortcut is useful only if every cost—including occasional large slippage—is represented in the average. Keep one unit throughout: bp is usually clearest.

9–13 · The execution funnel

A chart opportunity is progressively reduced as it passes through the trading process. This is why a backtest using candle closes and no fill model can look attractive while executable results do not.

Spread is the gap between the best displayed bid and ask. Crossing it for immediate execution creates a cost. A resting limit order may avoid crossing, but it introduces non-fill risk and adverse selection. A market order prioritises execution, not price; a limit order controls price, not certainty of execution. The SEC’s order-type explainer makes this trade-off explicit.

13–17 · Worked arithmetic

Suppose a paper setup has 20 outcomes: 12 wins and 8 losses. The hit rate is 60%. Average gross win is 14 bp; average gross loss is 9 bp.

Gross expectancy = (0.60 × 14) − (0.40 × 9)
= 8.4 − 3.6 = +4.8 bp per trade

Now estimate round-trip friction: 2 bp entry fee + 2 bp exit fee + 3 bp spread/slippage = 7 bp. Assume for this simple exercise that it applies equally to each completed trade.

Average net win = 14 − 7 = 7 bp
Average net loss = 9 + 7 = 16 bp
Net expectancy = (0.60 × 7) − (0.40 × 16)
= 4.2 − 6.4 = −2.2 bp per trade

The strategy wins more often than it loses and has positive gross expectancy, yet loses after costs. Its gross payoff ratio is 14 ÷ 9 = 1.56; its net payoff ratio collapses to 7 ÷ 16 = 0.44. At those net win/loss sizes, the break-even hit rate is 16 ÷ (7 + 16) ≈ 69.6%.

17–26 · Timed paper simulation

Open a liquid crypto market in replay mode or hide the right side of a historical chart. Do not place a live order. Use one execution style for the whole drill, record every valid signal, and do not add leverage.

  1. 2 minutes—specify: market, timeframe, exact setup, trigger, stop/invalidation, target and maximum holding time.
  2. 1 minute—cost model: copy the venue’s current published fee for your hypothetical order type; observe the spread; choose a conservative slippage allowance. Convert all to round-trip bp.
  3. 5 minutes—simulate: advance one candle at a time. Record intended price, hypothetical fill, exit, gross bp, friction bp and net bp. Count a limit order only if price actually trades through it; mark uncertain queue fills as “not filled.”
  4. 1 minute—stress: double the slippage component and recalculate. Do not rewrite the setup after seeing outcomes.

Use this compact row for every attempt:

time | setup valid? | intended entry | assumed fill | exit | gross bp | fees | spread/slip | net bp | fill confidence | rule break?
Interpretation limit: this drill tests process and arithmetic, not profitability. Twenty trades are far too few to establish an edge; correlated market conditions make the effective evidence smaller still.
