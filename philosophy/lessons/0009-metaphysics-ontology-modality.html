Lesson 9 · Metaphysics · About 20 minutes

Ask what exists—and what could have been

A worldview proposes both an inventory of reality and claims about reality’s structure. Modal reasoning tests a further dimension: which features are accidental, which are possible, and which could not have been otherwise.

Lesson 8 asked what, if anything, makes moral claims true. That question already touches metaphysics. Are there moral properties? Are they reducible to natural properties, constituted by practices, or fundamental? Metaphysics is the broader inquiry into reality’s basic character: existence, properties, identity, time, causation, composition, dependence, possibility, and necessity all fall within its shifting borders. Ontology focuses on what there is and what kinds of things there are. The distinction is useful rather than absolute. “Do mental events exist?” is ontological; “Are mental events grounded in physical events?” accepts both terms for discussion and asks about structure.

The difference prevents two common shortcuts. Naming something does not establish that it exists, and admitting that it exists does not establish that it is fundamental. A metaphysician might accept tables while holding that every fact about them is fully grounded in facts about particles or a wave function. The table remains in the inventory, but the theory assigns explanatory priority elsewhere. Likewise, two people can agree that conscious experiences occur while disagreeing whether they are identical with physical states, grounded in them, or irreducible.

Actual truth and modal status answer different questions

A true proposition may be necessary, meaning that it could not have been false, or contingent, meaning that it is true but could have been false. A false proposition may nevertheless be possible if it could have been true, or impossible if it could not. The SEP contrasts “Paris is the capital of France” with the proposition that there is a prime between every integer greater than one and its double. Both are true. The first could have been false; the mathematical proposition could not. Actual truth therefore does not by itself reveal necessity.

Modal logic adds two operators to the propositional machinery. □P means “necessarily P”; ◇P means “possibly P.” On the metaphysical reading, a possible world is a complete way reality might have been. To say □P is to say P is true at every possible world; to say ◇P is to say P is true at least one possible world. This talk need not picture worlds as planets, nor by itself commit you to David Lewis’s theory that possible worlds are concrete universes. Possible-world semantics is a model; philosophers disagree about the ontology, if any, that best explains it.

The operators are interdefinable. ◇P is equivalent to ¬□¬P: P is possible just when its negation is not necessary. □P is equivalent to ¬◇¬P: P is necessary just when its negation is impossible. These equivalences help translation, but the subscript silently missing from ordinary debate still matters: possible in what sense?

Logical, physical, practical, epistemic, deontic, and metaphysical modalities are not generally coextensive: changing the constraints changes which alternatives count as possible. On a common nesting model, every physically possible proposition is metaphysically possible, and every metaphysically possible proposition is logically possible, though philosophers dispute whether these modalities are genuinely distinct. The textbook’s running example says a human’s running at one hundred miles per hour is humanly impossible, might be technologically enabled with robotic legs, and differs again from travelling faster than light, which actual physical law forbids. A contradiction is logically impossible; a violation of actual physical law need not be logically contradictory. Likewise “Mark must be in the room, since I hear him” usually expresses an epistemic conclusion about what the evidence supports, not a claim that Mark occupies that room in every metaphysically possible world.

Ask whether necessity belongs to a proposition or a thing

De dicto modality is modality of what is said: an entire proposition falls within the scope of “necessarily” or “possibly.” De re modality attributes a modal feature to a thing: Sally herself might not have existed, or she might have spoken only French. On that account Sally exists contingently and speaks English accidentally. If she has a property she could not exist without, that property is essential to her.

Example provenance. The Sally examples are adapted from “Metaphysics,” §3.1. Changes: converted Sally’s first-person statements into third-person reports and shortened the source’s definitions of contingent existence and accidental properties.

The difference becomes sharp when descriptions can pick out different things in other circumstances. Suppose someone has exactly three sisters. “Necessarily, the number of that person’s sisters is greater than two” has a de dicto reading under which the description remains inside necessity: it says the person could not have had fewer sisters, which is doubtful. A de re reading first picks out the actual number, three, and says of that number that it is necessarily greater than two, which is true on the standard understanding of arithmetic. The same words can therefore conceal different scopes.

Worldview arguments often turn on modal premises. “Necessarily, God exists” is not merely “God exists with great certainty.” Read de dicto, it says the proposition that God exists is true at every possible world; a de re formulation would first pick out God and attribute necessary existence to that being. An argument must make clear which scope it needs. “The mind could exist without the body” is a possibility claim used in arguments for dualism. “Moral truths could not have been otherwise” assigns necessity to morality. In each case, notation can expose the commitment but cannot establish it. A modal argument still owes a reason for its possibility or necessity premise.

Conceivability may begin that argument, but imagination without constraint is unreliable: overlooking a hidden contradiction can make an impossible situation seem coherent. The opposite error is to infer necessity from familiarity—because the world has always appeared one way to us, it could not have been otherwise. A disciplined modal audit therefore identifies the kind of modality, fixes what remains constant across the proposed alternatives, states whether the claim is de re or de dicto, and asks what licenses the move from conceivability, essence, logic, or empirical knowledge to metaphysical possibility.

Dialogue challenge

Choose one claim: God exists necessarily; the mind could exist without the body; moral truths could not have been otherwise; or a modal claim central to your own worldview. State exactly which kind of possibility or necessity you mean. Say whether the modality applies de dicto to a proposition or de re to a thing or property. For a possibility claim, describe a candidate world that would witness it; for a necessity claim, describe a candidate counterexample world that would refute it. State what remains fixed there, and identify the premise that licenses treating your description as genuinely possible. I will challenge the modal bridge rather than merely asserting the opposite conclusion. Ask follow-up questions about anything unclear.

A careful worldview says what it admits, what it treats as fundamental, and what it claims could or could not have been otherwise. Modal vocabulary marks those commitments; it does not prove them.
