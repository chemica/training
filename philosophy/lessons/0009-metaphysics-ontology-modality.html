Lesson 9 · Metaphysics · 10–15 minutes

Map reality beyond the actual

Learn four questions that worldviews often blur. What exists? What is identical to what? What depends on what? What could or must have been?

A worldview is more than a list of beliefs. It includes an inventory of reality and a proposed architecture. Are minds identical to brains? Do moral facts depend on human attitudes? Could the universe have failed to exist? Each question needs a different tool.

Metaphysics is the map. Ontology is the inventory

Metaphysics studies the most general structure of reality. Its questions concern identity, time, causation, composition, possibility, dependence, properties, persons, and much else. Ontology is the part of metaphysics concerned especially with what exists and the categories into which existents fall. The boundary is debated, so treat this as a useful working distinction rather than a decree. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s overview of metaphysics explains how the field’s scope has expanded.

ONTOLOGYWhat entities or kinds must our best account admit? IDENTITYWhen are apparently different descriptions or objects really one? DEPENDENCEWhat exists or obtains in virtue of something else? MODALITYWhat is possible, necessary, impossible, or contingent?

Compare two claims. “Pain exists” is ontological. “Pain is identical to neural activity” adds a metaphysical identity claim. “Pain is grounded in neural activity” instead proposes an asymmetric explanatory structure. These are not interchangeable.

Identity is not resemblance

Numerical identity means being one and the same entity. Clark Kent resembles Superman perfectly because Clark Kent is Superman. Two identical-looking coins are qualitatively alike but numerically distinct. In symbols, a = b says that the object named by a is the very object named by b.

A governing principle is often called Leibniz’s Law. If a = b, then whatever is true of a is true of b. Apparent counterexamples often reveal a difference in description, belief, time, or context rather than two properties of one object. The SEP entry on the identity of indiscernibles distinguishes this principle from the controversial converse that perfectly indiscernible things must be identical.

Identity claim. A mental state just is a physical state.

Dependence claim. A mental state exists because of, or is grounded in, physical states.

Correlation claim. Mental and physical states reliably vary together.

Identity is the strongest of these claims. If pain is identical to neural state N, there are not two things with a bridge between them. There is one state under two descriptions. Evidence for correlation alone cannot establish that.

Dependence and grounding ask what is prior

Ontological dependence names a family of relations in which one entity depends on another for its existence or identity. Grounding is often used more narrowly for non-causal metaphysical explanation. If a truth is grounded in other facts, those facts make it obtain or explain why it obtains at a more fundamental level. Philosophers dispute the exact relation between grounding and dependence, so do not treat either as a magic word. See the SEP survey of ontological dependence.

A grounding claim is not ordinarily a causal history. The fact that a particular set has Socrates as its sole member may be grounded in Socrates and the membership relation, but Socrates did not manufacture the set earlier in time. Likewise, a moral naturalist might say that an act’s wrongness is grounded in natural facts about suffering and agency. A theist might say contingent reality ultimately depends on God. An atheist might instead propose fundamental physical reality, necessary laws, or perhaps no deeper ground at all. Every proposal incurs questions about what is fundamental and why the dependence terminates there.

Ask whether a speaker means causation, correlation, identity, existential dependence, or grounding. “Depends on” is often hiding the argument.

Modal logic reaches beyond actuality

Modality concerns ways a claim can be true or false, not merely whether it is actually true. Four basic statuses matter.

NECESSARYIt could not have been false. POSSIBLEIt could have been true. IMPOSSIBLEIt could not have been true. CONTINGENTIt is possible but not necessary. It could have been otherwise.

Modal logic adds operators to propositional logic. □P, read “necessarily P,” says P is true in every relevant possible world. ◇P, read “possibly P,” says P is true in at least one relevant possible world. Thus ◇P is equivalent to ¬□¬P. Possibly P means that it is not necessary that not-P.

A possible world is a complete way reality might have been. In basic semantics it functions like a complete evaluation context. You need not imagine a distant planet or commit to concrete parallel universes. Philosophers disagree about what possible worlds ultimately are. The semantics can still model modal reasoning by treating necessity and possibility as quantification over worlds. See the SEP entry on possible worlds.

Developer analogy. Ordinary truth asks whether an assertion passes in the current runtime state. Possibility asks whether it passes in at least one permitted state. Necessity asks whether it passes in every permitted state. The hard metaphysical question is what counts as a permitted state.

Accessibility makes “every world” precise

A basic Kripke model contains a set of worlds, a valuation saying which atomic propositions are true at each world, and an accessibility relation. From a world w, □P is true when P is true at every world accessible from w. ◇P is true when P is true at some accessible world. Different constraints on accessibility generate different modal logics. Saul Kripke’s early work made this approach central. His 1963 paper “Semantical Considerations on Modal Logic” is the primary source.

For metaphysical possibility, philosophers commonly reason as if any genuinely possible world is relevant. Other modalities change the relation. In epistemic logic, accessible worlds might be those compatible with what an agent knows. In deontic logic, they might represent normatively acceptable alternatives. So the box and diamond do not explain themselves. Always ask, “Necessary or possible in what sense?”

Countermodel challenge

At the actual world, P is true. At one accessible world, P is false. Which verdict follows there?

Where worldviews meet modal pressure

Modal language drives philosophy of religion. Cosmological arguments may distinguish contingent beings from a necessary foundation. Modal ontological arguments reason from a possible necessary being toward actual existence, but their force depends on the modal system and on whether the key possibility premise is justified. Arguments from evil may ask whether a perfectly good, powerful, knowing God could coexist with certain evils. Naturalists also make modal commitments when they claim consciousness must, could, or could not arise from physical organisation.

A modal argument cannot create possibility by definition. Conceivability may be evidence of possibility, but it is not automatically decisive. Nor does actuality alone reveal necessity. To evaluate a worldview claim, expose its modal premise and search for a coherent countermodel.

Dialogue challenge. Modal audit

Choose one claim. “God exists necessarily.” “Only physical things exist.” “Moral truths could not have been otherwise.” Or supply your own. Send me five short parts.

  1. State the claim without ambiguity.
  2. Classify it as ontological, identity-based, grounding-based, modal, or a combination.
  3. Translate its modal component using box or diamond.
  4. Describe one possible world that would test it.
  5. Name the premise an opponent should challenge first.

I will test whether your imagined world is genuinely coherent, identify any hidden shift between identity and dependence, and argue the strongest opposing case. Ask follow-up questions about anything unclear.
