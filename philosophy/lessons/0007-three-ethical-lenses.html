Lesson 7 · Normative ethics · About 25 minutes

Ask what each moral lens makes visible

Consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics organise moral attention differently. Today you will use all three on one hard case, without pretending that each tradition is a slogan or that agreement among them removes the need for judgment.

Normative ethics asks how we ought to act, which dispositions are virtues, and what makes lives or institutions go well. It is not a poll of what people happen to approve; that would be descriptive inquiry. Nor is it yet the metaethical question of what words such as “ought” mean or whether moral judgments can be true. The practical aim is to deliberate better. A theory earns its place here by directing attention to reasons you might otherwise miss and by helping you explain, test, and revise a decision.

The three approaches are better understood as families. Consequentialists can disagree about what is valuable, whose good counts, whether to assess acts or rules, and how uncertainty should affect choice. Deontologists can disagree about the basis and stringency of duties and rights. Virtue ethicists can disagree about human flourishing and the virtues it requires. Even their boundaries are contested. The lesson therefore gives you three disciplined questions, not three automatic verdicts. What would result? What is owed? What would excellent practical judgment express?

One case, three patterns of moral attention

In the 1884 case Regina v Dudley and Stephens, two shipwrecked sailors deliberately killed Richard Parker, a weak and unresisting cabin boy, and fed on his body. The jury found that without doing so the men probably would not have survived, and that Parker was likely to die before them. Rescue might have arrived the next day, or might not have arrived at all. The court nevertheless held that necessity did not legally justify the killing.

Pause before applying a theory. What exactly is the choice being assessed: the killing, a proposed rule permitting such killings, the selection of Parker, or the character displayed under extreme pressure? What facts were available to the sailors at the time, rather than visible in hindsight? Who receives the benefit, who bears the cost, and who gets to decide? Moral theories often seem to conflict because people have silently framed different acts or assumed different facts. Good deliberation begins by making those choices explicit.

A consequentialist asks which available act, policy, or rule produces the best outcome according to the relevant account of value. In chapter two of Utilitarianism, John Stuart Mill says that actions are right insofar as they tend to promote happiness and wrong insofar as they tend to produce its reverse. Mill’s standard is impartial: it is not the agent’s happiness alone but happiness altogether. That already rules out the caricature “do what benefits me.” It also makes the Dudley and Stephens case harder than the arithmetic “one dies, three live.” A serious assessment must consider probabilities known at the time, Parker’s loss, each survivor’s prospects, fear and suffering, alternative distributions of risk, and the longer-run effects of allowing endangered people to select the weakest person for sacrifice.

Consequences matter at more than one level. An act consequentialist assesses each particular act by its consequences; when deciding under uncertainty, many versions compare expected consequences, though theories differ over whether the ultimate criterion concerns expected or actual results. One influential kind of rule consequentialism asks which generally accepted rule would have the best consequences. Both can care about trust, justice, and rights because respecting them often has profound effects; the disagreement is whether those considerations matter ultimately because of consequences or independently of them. Nor does consequentialism require fake precision. When values resist a common unit, state the trade-off. When probabilities are obscure, give a range and ask whether the recommendation changes across it. When a benefit is spread widely but a harm is concentrated on one vulnerable person, make that distribution visible instead of hiding it inside a total.

A deontologist holds that the moral status of an action is not exhausted by the value of its results. What we owe one another can constrain how good outcomes may be pursued. Kant supplies one influential version, not the definition of the whole family. His universal-law formulation asks whether one can will the maxim of one’s action as a universal law. A maxim is not a vague label such as “save lives”; it identifies what the agent proposes to do, in what circumstances, and for what end. Kant’s own false-promise case makes the method concrete. A person in financial distress proposes to borrow by promising repayment while knowing repayment will not occur. Universalising that policy would undermine the practice of promising on which the deception depends.

Example provenance. Adapted from Kant’s public-domain false-promise example in Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals, second section. Changes: modernised and compressed the borrower’s circumstances, maxim, and Kant’s contradiction-in-conception analysis; no verdict or essential inferential step was changed.

Kant’s humanity formulation adds another question: does the action treat rational humanity, in oneself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means? “Merely” matters. Involving another person as a means to an end is not automatically forbidden; the defect lies in using that person on a maxim to which the person could not in principle consent, thereby bypassing rational agency. Applied to the shipwreck, the sailors intentionally killed an unresisting person for the others’ survival without his assent. The court also worried that a permissive principle would leave those who benefit to decide whose life is least valuable. A Kantian analysis can therefore focus on coercion, non-consent, and the asymmetry of making the weakest bear the whole cost. Other deontologists may reach similar concerns through rights rather than Kant’s formulas, and they may disagree about whether rights are absolute, defeasible, or overridden in catastrophe.

The court’s concern about permitting beneficiaries to measure lives is adapted from its warning about who would judge necessity. Changes: connected these two public-domain passages to frame a Kantian analysis; the claim that deontologists differ over rights is contextual explanation, not attributed to either primary source.

Virtue ethics shifts attention from “Which rule applies?” alone to “What would it be to perceive, feel, choose, and act well here?” Aristotle says ethical inquiry is practical: we study virtue not merely to know what it is, but to become good. Character is formed through repeated action, so a decision is both an answer to the present and practice for the kind of person one becomes. This does not make right action whatever a supposedly virtuous person happens to fancy. Aristotle’s agent must act knowingly, choose the act for its own sake, and act from a firm character; practical wisdom is needed to see what the particular circumstances call for.

Theory provenance. Adapted from Aristotle’s public-domain Nicomachean Ethics II.2 on the practical purpose and necessary imprecision of ethics and II.4 on the conditions of virtuous action. Changes: combined and modernised the two passages; added the present-decision-as-practice explanation as an inference from Aristotle’s account of habituation.

The celebrated doctrine of the mean is easy to trivialise. Courage is concerned with fear and confidence and stands between cowardice and rashness, but the mean is not an arithmetic compromise and not every act has a virtuous middle quantity. Aristotle explicitly says that some actions are wrong in themselves rather than wrong only when excessive or deficient. The practical question is what response is fitting, toward the right objects, for the right reasons, at the right time, and in the right manner. In the shipwreck case, virtue ethics asks whether justice can permit placing all risk on an unconsenting boy, whether courage requires enduring danger rather than transferring it, whether compassion attends to every person rather than only the survivors, and whether practical wisdom can find an alternative that panic has hidden. It also insists on humane judgment of agents under terrible pressure. Understanding temptation is part of perceiving the case accurately; excusing whatever temptation produces is not.

Triangulate, locate the disagreement, then judge

Using three lenses is not a vote in which two theories defeat one. Agreement reached by different theoretical routes can increase confidence, but the theories may overlap in their reasons or inherit the same mistaken facts. Disagreement is even more informative. Ask whether it concerns the predicted outcome, the thing counted as valuable, a constraint on pursuing value, the description of the act, or the qualities of perception and character the situation demands. That diagnosis tells you what further evidence or argument would actually move the discussion.

Use a compact four-pass discipline. First, specify the act, alternatives, affected parties, information available at the time, and important uncertainties. Second, rotate: compare expected consequences and their distribution; identify duties, rights, maxims, consent, and forms of respect; then ask what the virtues and practical wisdom disclose. Third, locate exactly where the reasons converge or conflict. Finally, judge. Give a conclusion with calibrated confidence, name the strongest opposing reason, and state what factual or moral claim would change your mind. Ethical theory should make responsibility more articulate, not provide three vocabularies for avoiding a decision.

Method provenance. Original course method. It synthesises the three sourced approaches above but is not copied or adapted from an external example or test. “Specify, rotate, locate, judge” and the associated prompts are original.

Moral deliberation dialogue

Assess the killing in Regina v Dudley and Stephens morally, not merely legally. Send me four short paragraphs. First specify the act and the important alternatives and uncertainties. Then give the strongest consequentialist assessment, including both immediate and rule-level effects. Next give the strongest deontological and virtue-ethical assessments without assuming that either must condemn or excuse the act. Finally state your judgment, confidence from zero to one hundred, strongest opposing reason, and one changed fact or principle that would move you.

I will first reconstruct your reasoning charitably. Then I will challenge the weakest transition, argue the strongest rival position, and ask whether your principle survives a nearby variation. Ask follow-up questions about any distinction that remains unclear.

Test provenance. Adapted from the court’s facts and uncertainty in Regina v Dudley and Stephens. Changes: turns the historical case into a four-part philosophical deliberation; explicitly separates morality from the legal verdict; adds comparison across the three theories, confidence calibration, a rival reason, and a revision condition. No model answer is supplied because the exercise assesses reasoned application rather than allegiance to one verdict.

Do not ask which theory supplies the approved answer. Ask what each theory makes morally visible, where their reasons conflict, and which judgment you can defend without concealing its costs.
