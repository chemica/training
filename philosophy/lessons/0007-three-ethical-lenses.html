Lesson 7 · Normative ethics · 10–15 minutes

See a moral decision through three lenses

Consequences, duties, and character reveal different moral features. Learn to apply all three without reducing any of them to a slogan.

Your win for today

You will be able to analyse one realistic dilemma through consequentialist, deontological, and virtue-ethical lenses, identify where they agree, and explain where the real disagreement lies.

Normative ethics asks what to do

Normative ethics develops standards for action and character. It asks what we ought to do, what makes an act right, and what kind of person we should become. That differs from descriptive ethics, which studies what people happen to believe, and metaethics, which asks what moral claims mean and whether they can be true.

The three traditions in this lesson are families, not single formulas. Consequentialists disagree about which outcomes matter and how to assess rules. Deontologists disagree about duties, rights, and exceptions. Virtue ethicists disagree about the virtues and the shape of flourishing. The aim is not to pick a team in ten minutes. It is to notice morally relevant features your first instinct may conceal. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on deontological ethics, its entry on consequentialism, and its entry on virtue ethics map the internal diversity.

The case. Ship, stop, or disclose?

You lead a team maintaining a council benefits portal. Hours before a statutory deadline, you discover that its fraud model wrongly delays about two per cent of legitimate applications. Delay could leave vulnerable people without rent or food money. Stopping release would delay every application for several days. Your director says the error rate is “within tolerance” and tells you not to mention it publicly until after launch.

You can ship silently, stop the release, or propose a limited launch with human review, public disclosure, and emergency payments for delayed cases. The third option is feasible, but it will cost more and anger senior management.

Before reading on, make a provisional choice. Then name the fact that does most of the moral work for you. That separation matters. A conclusion can hide the principle that produced it.

Lens one. Consequences

Consequentialism assesses acts, rules, or policies by the value of their consequences. A utilitarian version treats everyone's well-being as morally relevant and seeks the best overall outcome. John Stuart Mill states the utilitarian standard in chapter two of his Utilitarianism. Yet consequentialism is not simply “do whatever helps the majority.” It requires counting harms to minorities, indirect effects, uncertainty, trust, precedent, and alternatives. Rule consequentialists ask which generally adopted rules would have the best results. Other consequentialists value things besides welfare.

Ask. What are the available actions? Who is affected? What benefits and harms are likely, how severe are they, and how confident am I? What happens if this decision becomes a precedent?

Applied. Shipping silently is fast but imposes concentrated, serious harms and may damage public trust. Stopping protects against wrongful delay but harms all applicants. A limited launch may minimise expected harm while preserving service, though its cost and operational risk belong in the calculation.

A competent analysis does not pretend that all values have a precise common unit. It makes estimates explicit, tests sensitivity, and asks whether a small probability of catastrophic harm outweighs a large probability of mild benefit.

Lens two. Duties and respect

Deontological ethics holds that some features of action matter independently of maximising good outcomes. Duties, rights, permissions, promises, and constraints may set limits on what we may do to people. In Kant's ethics, rational agents must be treated as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Read Kant's second formulation in the Project Gutenberg edition of the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals.

“Never lie, whatever happens” is not an adequate summary of all deontology. Duties can conflict. Disclosure may be owed to citizens, while privacy and professional obligations constrain what may be disclosed. Some deontologists regard rights as stringent but defeasible. Kantian reasoning also examines the maxim of an action. Could you coherently will the policy behind your act as a rule for everyone?

Ask. What do I owe each person? Whose rights or agency are at stake? Am I deceiving, coercing, exploiting, or using anyone merely as a tool? Could the policy behind my action be justified to everyone subject to it?

Applied. Concealing a known error denies applicants information relevant to challenging a decision. Treating wrongful delay as an acceptable statistic may subordinate particular people to administrative convenience. Human review and disclosure better respect their agency, even if secrecy would marginally improve aggregate efficiency.

Lens three. Character and practical wisdom

Virtue ethics centres excellent character and practical wisdom. Aristotle begins from eudaimonia. Often translated as flourishing, it is living and acting well across a complete life. Virtues such as justice, courage, honesty, and generosity are stable dispositions shaped by practice. In books two and six of the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle connects virtue with finding what is appropriate in circumstances and with practical wisdom.

Virtue ethics is not “do what a nice person would do.” A virtue can lie between opposed failures, but the mean is not a numerical midpoint. Courage responds fittingly to real danger. Too little can be cowardice. Too much can be rashness. Practical wisdom, or phronesis, perceives which features matter here and coordinates virtues when they pull in different directions.

Ask. What would a just, honest, courageous, and practically wise person notice? Which response expresses those virtues together? What habits and institutional culture will this choice cultivate?

Applied. Silence may display servility or indifference. Abruptly stopping everything may be rigid rather than wise. The limited launch could integrate compassion for applicants, honesty about risk, justice in review, and courage toward management. But motive matters. Choosing it merely to appear heroic would not display the same character.

Triangulate before you decide

The lenses can converge for different reasons. All three may support the limited launch. That agreement increases confidence, but it does not erase disagreement. Change the facts and the fault lines appear. Suppose secrecy would certainly prevent panic and save more people. A direct act consequentialist may favour concealment. A rights-focused deontologist may still reject it. A virtue ethicist asks whether honest practical wisdom could ever permit temporary silence.

Use this four-pass method.

  1. Specify. List feasible options, affected people, uncertainties, and constraints.
  2. Rotate. Ask the consequence, duty, and character questions before defending a conclusion.
  3. Locate. Identify convergence and the exact point of conflict. Is it about predicted facts, whose welfare counts, whether a right is absolute, or which trait is virtuous?
  4. Judge. Choose and explain what would change your mind. Do not hide behind “the theories disagree.”

Ethical lenses improve judgment when they expose neglected reasons. They become evasions when they are used merely to decorate a decision already made.

Final moral deliberation dialogue

Decide the council case. Send me six short parts.

  1. Your chosen action and confidence from zero to one hundred.
  2. The strongest consequentialist case for it.
  3. The strongest deontological case for or against it.
  4. The virtues and possible character failures involved.
  5. The strongest rival option, stated charitably.
  6. One changed fact that would reverse your decision.

I will challenge your factual assumptions, argue for the strongest rival, and test whether your principles remain stable when the stakes change. Ask me any follow-up question if a distinction remains unclear.

Primary reading

Compare three short passages. Read chapter two of Mill's Utilitarianism, the second formulation of Kant's categorical imperative in the Groundwork, and Aristotle on virtue and the mean in book two of the Nicomachean Ethics. Read for the question each thinker makes difficult to ignore.
