Lesson 10 · Philosophy of religion · 15–20 minutes

Audit a worldview without rigging the test

Map the strongest evidence offered for theism and atheism. Then compare whole explanations without confusing a valid inference with a well-supported premise.

Your win for today

You will be able to steelman major arguments on both sides, locate the premise carrying the real dispute, and conduct a provisional worldview audit rather than declare a winner from one slogan.

Lesson 9 separated actuality, possibility, necessity, and dependence. Those distinctions now do real work. Philosophy of religion is not merely “Does God exist?” It asks what God, nature, explanation, and evidence mean, and which total picture best accounts for what we observe. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s overview maps the field’s breadth.

First specify the hypotheses

Classical theism proposes one necessarily existing, personal source of all other reality, traditionally characterised as omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, and worthy of worship. Naturalism, in one common metaphysical sense, denies that reality contains anything supernatural and treats the natural world as causally closed or explanatorily sufficient. Atheism is the denial that any gods exist; it does not by itself entail naturalism, moral error theory, nihilism, materialism, or certainty.

Agnosticism can mean withholding belief because the evidence is insufficient, or the stronger claim that God’s existence is unknown or unknowable. The map also includes deism, pantheism, panentheism, process theism, finite-god views, polytheisms, religious non-realism, and traditions that do not centre a creator. An objection to a perfectly loving omnipotent God may leave some of these untouched. Evidence against one religion is not automatically evidence against every religious metaphysics.

Never debate “God versus science” in the abstract. Define two live hypotheses precisely enough that evidence could discriminate between them.

Retrieval check one

Someone rejects classical theism because perfect power and perfect goodness seem incompatible with horrendous suffering. What follows directly?

The positive theistic case. Three families

Cosmological and contingency arguments begin from dependent, caused, changing, or contingent reality and infer a first cause, necessary being, or ultimate ground. A Leibnizian version can be reconstructed this way.

P1Every contingent fact has an adequate explanation. P2That contingent reality exists is itself a contingent fact. P3Its adequate explanation cannot be wholly contingent reality. CContingent reality ultimately depends on something necessary.

If the premises are read strongly enough, the inference can be valid. That does not make it sound. The principle of sufficient reason in P1, the contingency of the totality in P2, and the move from a necessary ground to a personal, good God all require defence. “Who caused God?” may miss a version whose conclusion is an uncaused necessary being; the sharper reply asks why that terminus is possible and explanatory while a natural foundation or brute fact is not. Compare the SEP survey of cosmological arguments with Aquinas’s compact primary formulations in the Summa Theologiae, First Part, question 2, article 3.

Teleological and fine-tuning arguments claim that life-permitting physical conditions are more expected if purposive intelligence lies behind the cosmos than if those conditions arise under an unguided chance hypothesis. Their modern form is usually abductive rather than deductive.

EThe universe has apparently life-permitting laws, constants, and initial conditions. H1A purposive creator intended embodied conscious life. H2A naturalistic single-universe or multiverse process produced the observed conditions. TESTWhich hypothesis makes E less surprising, after accounting for priors, selection effects, and auxiliary assumptions?

The steelman is not “complex things need designers.” It is a likelihood comparison. Critics challenge whether probabilities over constants are well defined, whether observers could only observe compatible conditions, whether a multiverse changes the comparison, and whether a creator’s aims are independently predictable. Defenders reply that observer selection does not by itself explain why any observer-permitting domain exists. The SEP natural-theology survey presents the contemporary debate. Hume’s Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion is the classic primary interrogation of design reasoning.

Moral and religious-experience arguments begin closer to human life. A moral argument may claim that objective duties, reasons, or human worth fit better within a theistic reality. An argument from experience may treat apparently perceptual awareness of God as prima facie evidence unless defeated, or may compare theism and naturalism as explanations of widespread religious experience.

The strongest versions do not say atheists cannot be moral or that every experience is veridical. Their crucial premises concern the ground of normativity and the epistemic entitlement of experience. Critics offer secular moral realism, constructivism, evolutionary and cultural explanations, contradictory revelations, and known cognitive error. Defenders ask whether causal explanations defeat a belief without showing it unreliable, and whether naturalism can explain normativity rather than merely moral psychology. See the SEP entries on moral arguments for God and religious experience.

Retrieval check two

A valid contingency argument reaches “a necessary ground exists.” Which further step needs support before classical theism follows?

The atheistic case. Four pressure points

The problem of evil targets the fit between suffering and a perfectly powerful, knowing, and good God. Logical versions claim incompatibility. Contemporary evidential versions more modestly argue that the kinds, amount, distribution, or apparent pointlessness of suffering are less expected on classical theism than on a rival hypothesis. William Rowe’s influential 1979 paper gives a primary formulation of this evidential strategy. Read “The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism.”

Theistic replies include free-will defences, soul-making, goods requiring stable natural laws, afterlife compensation, and sceptical theism about our ability to judge God’s reasons. Each has a cost. A defence need only show compatibility to answer a logical argument; a theodicy aims to identify God’s actual reasons. Against an evidential argument, bare logical possibility is not enough. The response must affect the probability comparison. The SEP problem-of-evil survey carefully separates these tasks.

Divine hiddenness asks why a perfectly loving God would permit capable, non-resistant people to lack belief and therefore a conscious relationship with God. A compact version says that perfect love is always open to relationship, such openness entails no non-resistant nonbelief, non-resistant nonbelief occurs, and therefore no perfectly loving God exists. Disputes concern what love requires, whether relationship requires explicit belief now, whether apparently non-resistant nonbelief really occurs, and whether distance enables important goods. J. L. Schellenberg’s own survey of the hiddenness argument and the SEP entry map the debate.

Incoherence and attribute-tension arguments ask whether the divine package is jointly possible. Can omnipotence include doing the logically impossible? Can timelessness coexist with acting? Can perfect foreknowledge coexist with libertarian freedom? Can perfect goodness coexist with freedom to do otherwise? Sophisticated theists usually define omnipotence as power to actualise every broadly logically possible state of affairs, not the ability to make contradictions true. That blocks cheap paradoxes, but substantive tensions remain. The proper test is a clear set of definitions followed by a contradiction or countermodel, not a riddle about a stone.

Evidential naturalism compares whole theories. Naturalism may be credited with ontological economy, continuity with successful scientific explanation, religious diversity, hiddenness, suffering, and the dependence of mental life on brains. The steelman is not “science has not detected God, therefore no God.” It is that a naturalistic model may explain the total evidence with fewer kinds of entity and fewer special assumptions. Theists reply that naturalism may leave unexplained why anything exists, why laws are orderly, why consciousness and reason arise, and why normative truths bind us. Economy counts only alongside explanatory power and fit. Graham Oppy’s primary essay on naturalism illustrates a contemporary cumulative approach.

Retrieval check three

A free-will defence shows that God and some evil are logically compatible. What has it established against an evidential argument from apparently pointless suffering?

Validity is not premise support

A deductive formulation can be valid while its decisive premise is poorly justified. An inductive or abductive argument is not invalid merely because its conclusion remains uncertain. Keep three judgments apart.

FORMIf the premises were true, would the conclusion follow deductively? SUPPORTHow well does evidence justify each premise, likelihood, or prior? SCOPEWould the conclusion establish classical theism, some designer, atheism, naturalism, or only pressure on one view?

Then compare cumulative cases. Evidence can be mixed. Fine-tuning might favour theism relative to one naturalistic model while suffering favours naturalism relative to one theistic model. Auxiliary beliefs change predictions. A theist who expects soul-making differs from a theist who expects uninterrupted comfort. A naturalist committed to a multiverse differs from one committed to a single brute universe.

Beware double standards. Do not demand deductive proof from your opponent while accepting suggestive fit for yourself. Do not call your own unexplained foundation “basic” and your opponent’s “an evasion” without a principled difference. Do not count every possible rescue equally. A hypothesis that can accommodate anything predicts nothing unless its auxiliary assumptions are independently motivated.

Interleaved check

“Naturalism is simpler, so naturalism is true.” Which repair makes this a serious abductive argument?

A six-pass worldview audit

  1. Specify. Define the rival hypotheses. Include attributes, scope, and auxiliary commitments.
  2. Inventory. List the evidence before selecting favourite examples. Include existence, order, consciousness, reason, value, experience, suffering, hiddenness, diversity, and scientific success.
  3. Reconstruct. Put each argument into premises and conclusion. Mark it deductive, inductive, or abductive.
  4. Steelman. State each side in language an informed advocate could accept. Let neither side survive as a meme.
  5. Compare. Judge fit, scope, simplicity, coherence, and auxiliary assumptions. Track which evidence actually discriminates.
  6. Calibrate. Record confidence, unresolved tensions, and what observation or argument would move it.

A worldview audit is not neutral because it lacks commitments. It is fair because the same standards expose the costs of every live option, including your own.

Final mock debate. Audit your current worldview

Choose classical theism, naturalism, agnosticism, or a precisely named alternative as your current working view. Send me seven short parts.

  1. Define your view in two sentences and give confidence from zero to one hundred.
  2. Give its strongest positive argument in premise–conclusion form.
  3. Say whether that argument is deductive, inductive, or abductive.
  4. Name the premise with the weakest support.
  5. Steelman the strongest argument against your view.
  6. Name one cost your view accepts rather than explains away.
  7. State one finding or argument that would move your confidence by at least ten points.

I will take the strongest opposing position, question you for one round, then switch sides and improve your answer. We will score both cases for validity or inferential strength, premise support, scope, coherence, simplicity, and explanatory power. Ask me any follow-up question if a distinction remains unclear.

Primary reading

Read Hume’s Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, especially parts 2, 5, 10, and 11. Hume stages powerful voices rather than issuing a simple manifesto, which makes the work ideal practice in steelmanning. Pair it with Rowe’s “The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism”. Use the SEP philosophy-of-religion overview to locate both within the wider map.
