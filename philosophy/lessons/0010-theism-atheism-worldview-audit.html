Lesson 10 · Philosophy of religion · 25–35 minutes

Compare worldviews without rigging the comparison

The serious question is not which side has the most slogans. It is which precisely stated view best survives its strongest arguments, its strongest objections, and the costs it must honestly accept.

Your win for today

You will distinguish atheism, agnosticism, and naturalism; steelman representative theistic and atheistic arguments; use priors, likelihoods, and defeaters without pretending to mathematical precision; and produce a revisable audit of your own view.

Lesson 9 supplied the modal vocabulary this debate needs. A necessary being is not merely a very durable being, and a contingent universe need not have begun in time. Those distinctions prevent several cheap victories. This lesson also refuses a different shortcut. There is no single package called “the religious view” and no single package called “the scientific view.” Before evidence can discriminate, the alternatives must be stated.

Name the claim before judging it

In contemporary philosophy, theism ordinarily affirms that God exists, while atheism ordinarily affirms that God does not exist. In wider public use, “atheism” also often means simply lacking belief in God. These uses answer different questions. “Do you believe?” asks about a person’s psychological state; “Does God exist?” asks which proposition is true. Agnosticism can likewise name suspended judgment, or the epistemic claim that neither theism nor atheism is known or sufficiently justified. An agnostic in the first sense has not thereby selected a third kind of universe. They have reported their present attitude toward rival claims.

Naturalism is a further substantive view, not a synonym for atheism. Ontological naturalists differ among themselves, but a common commitment is that spatiotemporal reality is physically constituted and that physical effects have fully physical causes. An atheist may reject that picture by accepting irreducible minds, abstract objects, or non-natural moral facts. Conversely, methodological reliance on natural explanations in science does not by itself settle the ontology of everything. For this audit, we will compare classical theism with a working naturalist hypothesis, while remembering that neither exhausts logical space.

Scope matters too. An argument against an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good personal creator does not by itself refute deism, pantheism, a finite god, every polytheism, or every religious practice. Nor does an argument for a necessary foundation yet establish that the foundation knows, loves, commands, or deserves worship. A fair conclusion is no broader than its premises.

Two arguments that can support a theistic case

A contingency argument begins not with a temporal first event but with dependent existence. One detailed deductive version starts from the existence of something contingent, requires a complete explanation for every contingent being, denies that a contingent being can explain itself, and argues that contingent beings alone cannot supply the complete explanation. It concludes that a necessary being exists and, with further premises about the universe, that this being is other than the universe.

P1A contingent being exists. P2Every contingent being has a fully adequate explanation of its existence. P3A contingent being cannot explain its own existence. P4Its full explanation either uses contingent beings alone or includes a necessary being. P5Contingent beings alone cannot fully explain contingent beings. CTherefore, a necessary being exists.

The form makes the pressure points visible. Premise two invokes a version of the Principle of Sufficient Reason. A critic may instead permit a brute contingent fact. Premise five must explain why an infinite or total network of contingent explanations is inadequate. Most importantly, the conclusion above is only a necessary being. Personality, unity, knowledge, goodness, creative choice, and worship-worthiness require further argument. Asking “what caused the uncaused being?” misses the stipulated conclusion; asking why a necessary divine terminus is more intelligible than a necessary natural foundation or a brute cosmos meets the argument where it is strongest.

Fine-tuning arguments are better treated as comparative rather than deductive. Let E be the existence of observer-permitting laws, constants, and initial conditions. Let T include a creator who has reason to produce embodied conscious life, and let N be a specified naturalistic model. E favours T over N only if E is more expected given T than given N. That is not yet the posterior verdict. We must also ask how antecedently plausible each hypothesis is, whether its auxiliary assumptions were independently motivated, and whether observation selection distorts the comparison.

Elliott Sober’s net analogy isolates the selection issue. You pull a net from a lake and every fish in a large catch is longer than ten inches. “This lake has only large fish” looks like one explanation. Learning that the net cannot retain smaller fish undercuts that inference: the sampling process made small fish unobservable. Likewise, observers could not find themselves in conditions incompatible with observers. Yet a selection effect is not automatically a complete naturalistic explanation of why there is any observer-permitting domain. Multiverse proposals, probability distributions over constants, and claims about divine purposes each need their own support. The lesson is not that selection effects settle fine-tuning. It is that evidence must be evaluated through the process by which it became observable.

Two arguments that can support an atheistic case

The problem of evil is strongest when its target and force are stated modestly. A logical argument alleges that God and evil are incompatible. An evidential argument can grant compatibility yet argue that some suffering is much less expected if a perfectly good, powerful, and knowing God exists than if no such God exists. William Rowe’s famous case asks us to consider an apparently pointless instance of intense animal suffering. The argument needs more than the fact that we cannot see a justifying reason. It needs support for moving from “no reason is apparent to us” toward “probably there is no adequate reason.”

A free-will defence can block a strict claim that God and moral evil are incompatible if it supplies a coherent possible story covering the evil targeted by that claim. A story in which God and merely some evil coexist establishes only that narrower compatibility. Neither result alone shows that free will explains animal suffering, natural disaster, the distribution of suffering, or the observed quantity of apparently gratuitous suffering. Here the distinction between a defence and a theodicy earns its place. A defence aims at possible compatibility; a theodicy proposes God’s actual reasons. Against an evidential argument, a reply must alter the likelihood comparison, not merely produce any logically possible story.

Divine hiddenness presses a different prediction of classical personal theism. Schellenberg’s argument says that some people are capable of relationship with God yet, through no fault of their own, lack belief; an unsurpassably great personal God would allow no such people; therefore no such God exists. The crucial premise connects perfect love with openness to conscious relationship. Critics question whether every perfectly loving God must make relationship available in that way and at every time, whether genuine non-resistance occurs, or whether our ignorance of divine reasons undercuts the inference. Defenders answer that conscious relationship cannot even begin without belief that the other party exists, and that proposed goods might be achieved within relationship rather than through its absence.

From isolated arguments to a cumulative case

Arguments rarely arrive alone. A theist may cite contingency, intelligible order, consciousness, moral normativity, and religious experience. A naturalist may cite scientific continuity, the apparent causal closure of the physical domain, evil, hiddenness, religious diversity, and the dependence of mental life on brains. A cumulative case asks how well a whole hypothesis accounts for the whole body of evidence. It should not merely count items. Two arguments may depend on the same disputed principle, and two observations may be expected for the same reason. Counting correlated evidence twice creates an illusion of accumulation.

Bayesian language can discipline the comparison even when exact numbers would be theatre. A prior records a hypothesis’s plausibility relative to background information before adding the particular evidence now under discussion. A likelihood asks how expected that evidence would be if the hypothesis and relevant background assumptions were true. Evidence favours T over N when it is more likely on T than on N; the posterior comparison also depends on priors and the rest of the evidence. “Fine-tuning is surprising on my version of N” and “suffering is surprising on my version of T” can both be true. The task is to revise the total comparison, not to insist that one item must cancel the other.

Defeaters help identify what changed. A rebutter supports the opposite or an incompatible claim. An undercutter attacks the connection between evidence and conclusion. Reliable records showing that a reported event did not occur would rebut the event claim. Evidence that the report was fabricated or that its witness was guessing instead undercuts the report’s evidential force; neither fact by itself proves that the event did not occur. The same discipline applies on both sides. An evolutionary explanation of why humans form religious beliefs does not automatically show those beliefs false; it must show the process is unreliable or remove the reason the experience supplied. A possible divine reason for suffering does not automatically make the observed suffering likely.

Audit from somewhere, but audit fairly

No one evaluates these arguments from nowhere. Your sense of simplicity, your trust in testimony, your standards for explanation, and your prior view of what reality is like all affect the result. The answer is not to hide those commitments under the word “neutral.” Make them inspectable. A naturalist may treat causal continuity with science as a strong prior virtue; a theist may treat the existence and intelligibility of a contingent cosmos as demanding personal explanation. Each must say why that standard is truth-conducive and apply comparable scrutiny to favoured and unfavoured explanations.

Steelman before scoring. State the target hypothesis precisely. Reconstruct the argument so an informed advocate recognises it. Identify whether it is deductive or comparative. Mark the premise, prior, or likelihood doing the real work. Then give the strongest objection, not the easiest one. Finally, distinguish an answer that rebuts a premise from one that merely undercuts its support, and distinguish a cost the worldview explains from one it simply accepts. Steelmanning is not concession. It is experimental control for argument.

1Specify. Define two live hypotheses and their auxiliaries. 2Inventory. List favourable, adverse, and awkward evidence before scoring. 3Reconstruct. Expose the inference and the load-bearing assumptions. 4Steelman. Use a formulation an informed opponent could own. 5Compare. Judge fit, scope, coherence, economy, priors, and auxiliary costs. 6Calibrate. Record confidence, costs, and what would change your mind.

Capstone dialogue. Put your worldview at risk

Choose classical theism, metaphysical naturalism, suspended judgment, or another precisely defined position. Send me the following seven parts.

  1. Your working view and confidence from zero to one hundred.
  2. The strongest positive case for it, including its load-bearing premise or likelihood.
  3. The strongest case against it, stated as its advocate would state it.
  4. One rebutting defeater and one undercutting defeater relevant to either case.
  5. One explanatory success and one cost your view currently accepts.
  6. One prior judgment that materially affects your verdict, with your reason for using it.
  7. One argument or finding that would move your confidence by at least ten points.

I will first argue from the strongest rival position, then switch sides and improve your case. We will check scope, inferential strength, premise support, explanatory fit, dependence between evidence, and whether the same standards were applied both ways.

Exercise provenance. This dialogue is an original exercise built from the original six-pass audit. Its philosophical distinctions are those explicitly sourced above; no historical example or published test is reproduced.

A deliberate worldview is not one with no unanswered questions. It is one whose claims, evidence, rivals, costs, and revision conditions you can state without protecting your own side from the standards used against the other.
