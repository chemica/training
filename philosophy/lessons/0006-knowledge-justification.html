Lesson 6 · Epistemology · About 20 minutes

Being right is not yet knowing

A belief can be true by accident, supported by evidence you ignored, or reasonable until new information undermines it. Learning to distinguish those possibilities is part of building a worldview responsibly.

The previous lessons examined arguments: their form, premises, and conclusions. Epistemology also examines the believer. When you say that you know God exists, that consciousness depends on brains, or that cruelty is wrong, you claim more than that the sentence happens to be true. You claim an appropriate connection to its truth. That connection matters because a lucky believer and a careful inquirer may reach the same answer while displaying very different epistemic achievements.

A familiar starting point analyses knowledge as justified true belief, abbreviated JTB. A subject S knows a proposition P if and only if P is true, S believes P, and S is justified in believing P. Each condition excludes a different failure. A false claim cannot be known merely because somebody confidently accepts it. A truth you do not believe is not something you know. A baseless guess does not become knowledge merely by landing correctly.

TRUTHP is true. BELIEFS believes that P. JUSTIFICATIONS is justified in believing that P.

Definition provenance. Adapted from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “The Analysis of Knowledge,” §1. Changes: reformatted the source’s numbered analysis as a card and omitted its biconditional heading. The order and wording of the three conditions are unchanged.

The justification condition is not normally a demand that you have performed a public defence of every belief. You can know that five plus five equals ten without having written a philosophical argument for it. Here “justified” means roughly that the belief is appropriately supported or formed. Philosophers disagree about what makes support appropriate: accessible reasons, reliable belief-forming processes, intellectual virtues, or some combination. The lesson’s practical question is narrower. What is the route from your evidence to your belief, and is that route one you should trust?

Good evidence must be the basis of the belief

There is a difference between having a good reason available and believing because of that reason. The SEP illustrates it with Ingrid. She possesses excellent evidence that a neighbourhood is dangerous, ignores it, and comes to believe the neighbourhood is dangerous only because a black cat crosses the street. Ingrid has what philosophers call propositional justification: there is good reason available for the claim. Her actual belief lacks doxastic justification because it is based on superstition rather than that evidence.

Example provenance. Adapted from the Ingrid example in “The Analysis of Knowledge,” §1.3.3. Changes: compressed the case and named the contrast before explaining it; the evidence, black-cat basis, and diagnosis are retained.

This distinction stops inquiry from becoming a post-hoc debate exercise. You may be able to produce clever reasons for a worldview commitment after being challenged, yet those reasons might not explain why you hold it. That does not automatically make the belief false, but it should change the audit. Ask which experiences, testimony, inferences, habits, loyalties, or fears actually sustain the belief. Then ask whether those bases deserve the influence they have. The basing relation—the relation between a reason and a belief held for that reason—is itself philosophically contested, but the practical distinction is indispensable.

Gettier cases reveal truth reached through luck

JTB initially looks capable of excluding both error and guessing. Edmund Gettier’s 1963 counterexamples showed why many philosophers think it is still insufficient. In one case, Smith has strong but misleading evidence that Jones owns a Ford. Smith validly infers the disjunction “Either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona,” although he has no idea where Brown is. Jones does not own a Ford; by sheer coincidence, Brown is in Barcelona. Smith therefore believes the disjunction, it is true, and his inference preserves the justification he had. Yet his evidence connected him to the false half of the disjunction, while luck supplied the truth of the other half. The natural verdict is that Smith did not know.

Example provenance. Adapted from Gettier’s second case as reproduced in “The Analysis of Knowledge,” §3, which cites Gettier 1963, p. 123. Changes: selected only the Barcelona disjunction, paraphrased the narrative, and made the role of the false evidence and coincidental truth explicit.

The lesson is not that justification is useless, nor that knowledge requires certainty. The case targets sufficiency: truth, belief, and justification can apparently all be present while epistemic luck prevents knowledge. Proposed repairs include excluding false steps, requiring a reliable method, requiring the belief to be safe from easy error, or treating knowledge as more basic than justification. No repair commands universal agreement. For present purposes, Gettier supplies an audit question: could this method easily have delivered the same belief when it was false, or did the belief become true in a way disconnected from the evidence?

Information acquired later can also change whether your original support remains adequate. A rebutting defeater gives evidence against the proposition itself. An undercutting defeater attacks the connection between your evidence and the proposition without directly supporting its negation. The SEP’s pilot example distinguishes them. The pilot has calculated that the aircraft carries enough fuel to fly another fifty miles. Seeing fuel leak from the tank rebuts that conclusion. By contrast, seeing on the altimeter that the aircraft is flying high enough for hypoxia to impair judgment gives the pilot reason to doubt whether she performed the calculation or assessed its support correctly. That evidence undercuts her route to the fuel belief without itself showing that the tank lacks enough fuel.

Example provenance. Adapted from the fuel-calculation and hypoxia setup and the later rebutter–undercutter comparison in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Epistemic Self-Doubt,” §3. Changes: combined and compressed the two passages and explicitly stated what each defeater targets.

A defeater is not any doubt you can imagine. It must be evidence with genuine bearing on the claim or its support. Nor is defeat always permanent: further evidence may defeat the defeater. Responsible belief therefore involves responsiveness rather than ritual scepticism. State the claim precisely, distinguish the evidence you possess from the basis you actually use, seek serious rebutters and undercutters, and adjust confidence when the total evidence changes. The result may be continued belief, suspended judgment, or rejection. What matters is that the movement answers to reasons rather than protecting an identity at all costs.

Dialogue challenge

Choose one belief that genuinely shapes your worldview. State the proposition and your confidence in it; give the strongest evidence you possess; say which consideration actually sustains the belief; identify one serious candidate rebutter and one serious candidate undercutter, or explain why you cannot find one; then construct a Gettier-like scenario in which the belief is true but your route reaches it through luck. I will separate challenges to the proposition from challenges to your route and press the strongest weak point. Ask follow-up questions about anything unclear.

Do not ask only whether a belief is true. Ask whether your actual route to it is appropriately supported, resistant to relevant defeaters, and connected to the truth in more than a lucky way.
