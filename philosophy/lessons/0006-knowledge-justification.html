Lesson 6 · Epistemology · 10–15 minutes

When a true belief is not enough

A worldview can contain true claims for bad reasons. Learn to separate belief, truth, and justification, then test whether luck or a defeater has broken the connection.

Earlier lessons taught you to examine how premises support a conclusion. Epistemology turns the lens on the thinker. What makes a person reasonable in believing a claim? When does reasonable belief become knowledge? These questions matter whenever you say, “I know there is a God,” “I know morality is objective,” or “I know consciousness is physical.”

Three different achievements

BELIEFYou take a proposition to be true. This is a psychological relation between you and a claim. TRUTHThe proposition is actually true. Sincerity and confidence cannot make it so. JUSTIFICATIONYou have epistemically good support for believing it, such as evidence, sound reasoning, or a suitably reliable basis.

These can come apart. You may believe a falsehood with excellent evidence. You may guess a truth with no evidence. You may possess evidence but believe for an unrelated reason. Philosophers call that last issue the basing relation. Evidence normally justifies your belief only when the belief is appropriately based on it. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s entry on the epistemic basing relation surveys competing accounts.

Ask three questions separately. Do I believe it? Is it true? What makes my belief reasonable?

Retrieval check one

Mira believes her train leaves at nine because she dreamed it. The train really does leave at nine. Which description is best?

The attractive model. Justified true belief

A historically influential analysis says that a subject knows a proposition when three conditions hold.

1The proposition is true. 2The subject believes the proposition. 3The subject is justified in believing the proposition.

This model explains why a confident mistake is not knowledge, why an unaccepted truth is not your knowledge, and why a lucky guess is not knowledge. It is an excellent checklist. But in 1963 Edmund Gettier argued that the three conditions are not sufficient. His paper is only three pages long. Read the original publication, “Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?”, or this accessible copy of Gettier’s paper.

Gettier pressure. Right answer, wrong route

Here is a modern case with the same structure. Ravi checks a normally reliable office status dashboard. It says Ana is online, but the dashboard is frozen on yesterday’s data. Unknown to Ravi, Ana has just logged in from her phone.

BELIEFRavi believes Ana is online. TRUTHAna really is online. JUSTIFICATIONRavi consulted a dashboard that is normally reliable. LUCKThe displayed evidence is disconnected from what makes the belief true.

Many people judge that Ravi does not know Ana is online. His justification points through a false picture of the situation, while a coincidence makes his conclusion true. This is epistemic luck. Gettier-style cases therefore pressure the claim that justified true belief is sufficient for knowledge. They do not show that belief, truth, or justification is irrelevant, and philosophers still disagree about the best repair. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s survey of Gettier problems traces proposed responses.

Retrieval check two

Which feature makes the dashboard case Gettier-like?

Evidence can be defeated

A defeater is information that removes or reduces the justification you would otherwise have. Two kinds are especially useful.

Rebutting defeater. It supports the opposite conclusion. Your weather app predicts sunshine, but you see a wall of storm clouds approaching.

Undercutting defeater. It attacks the connection between your evidence and conclusion. You learn that the weather station’s sensor is malfunctioning. That does not show it will rain. It shows that this reading is a poor guide.

A defeater can itself be misleading or defeated. The goal is not to invent any imaginable doubt. It is to look for a serious reason, given your total evidence, to distrust the claim or the route by which you reached it. Contemporary epistemology contains several competing accounts of justification. Some emphasise reasons accessible to the believer. Others emphasise reliable processes, intellectual virtues, or proper relations to the truth. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s overview of epistemology maps these debates.

Retrieval check three

You trust a historical manuscript, then learn that its date was assigned by a contaminated laboratory sample. What have you acquired?

A worldview audit in five passes

Choose one commitment that matters to you. Avoid starting with your entire worldview. Use one proposition, such as “moral facts exist” or “religious experience is evidence of a transcendent reality.”

  1. State the belief precisely. What proposition do you actually accept, and with what confidence?
  2. List your evidence. Include experience, testimony, arguments, and background assumptions.
  3. Check the basis. Which evidence genuinely causes or sustains your belief? Which reasons are merely available after the fact?
  4. Search for defeaters. What supports the opposite claim? What undermines your evidence-producing method?
  5. Run the luck test. Could your belief easily be true while your route to it is badly disconnected from the truth?

This audit does not require certainty before belief. Much responsible inquiry operates with degrees of confidence. Its purpose is calibration. A person who can name the evidence, defeaters, and conditions for revision has a more resilient worldview than someone who merely feels sure.

Interleaved check

A debater gives a valid argument for a conclusion that happens to be true, but its only substantive premise is false. Which diagnosis is most precise?

Dialogue challenge. Audit one live belief

Choose one claim that genuinely shapes your worldview. Prefer a claim about God, morality, consciousness, freedom, or what makes a life meaningful. Send me five short parts.

  1. State the claim and your confidence from zero to one hundred.
  2. Give your strongest evidence for it.
  3. Say whether that evidence actually formed your belief or was found later.
  4. Give one rebutting defeater and one undercutting defeater.
  5. Describe a Gettier-like way you might be right for the wrong reason.

I will distinguish attacks on the claim from attacks on your route to it, then argue for the position opposite yours. Ask follow-up questions about anything unclear.

Primary reading

Read Edmund Gettier’s “Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?”. It is unusually short and rewards reconstructing each case in belief, truth, justification, and luck terms. For orientation, use the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on epistemology.

Mission link. Build a worldview that is not merely true by accident, but responsive to evidence, defeaters, and the quality of the route from reasons to belief.
