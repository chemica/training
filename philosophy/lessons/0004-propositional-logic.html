Lesson 4 · Propositional logic · 10–15 minutes

Compile a claim before you test it

Natural language hides structure. Learn to translate claims into symbols so that scope, alternatives, and conditional commitments become visible.

Lesson 3 asked what kind of support an argument promises. Here we focus on deductive arguments and give their sentences a precise representation.

Your win for today

You will be able to symbolise ordinary claims using negation, conjunction, disjunction, conditionals, and biconditionals—and avoid reversing necessary and sufficient conditions.

Start with atomic propositions

An atomic proposition is a complete claim treated as having no smaller truth-functional parts. Assign each one a capital letter and record a key.

GGod exists.MObjective moral facts exist.SSuffering has a morally sufficient reason.

The letter is arbitrary. Its definition is not. “God” is not a proposition because it is not true or false; “God exists” is. Keep each key stable within an argument. The method and symbols here follow the truth-functional logic chapters of forall x: Calgary.

Five connectives build molecular claims

¬ANot A. Negation is true when A is false.A ∧ BA and B. Conjunction requires both.A ∨ BA or B. Inclusive disjunction requires at least one.A → BIf A, then B. The conditional rules out A being true while B is false.A ↔ BA if and only if B. The biconditional requires both directions.

In classical propositional logic, or is inclusive unless context says otherwise. So A ∨ B allows A alone, B alone, or both. An exclusive “either, but not both” needs more structure. (A ∨ B) ∧ ¬(A ∧ B).

These connectives track truth-functional structure. They do not by themselves represent causes, explanations, obligations, time, probability, or metaphysical necessity. Formalisation is a model of what matters for the inference, not a transcription of every shade of meaning. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s “Classical Logic” situates this approach within the broader study of logical consequence.

Retrieval check one

Let R mean “revelation occurs” and E mean “evidence exists.” Translate “Revelation does not occur, but evidence exists.”

Scope is where the bugs hide

Parentheses show which connective governs which part. Compare these claims.

¬(G ∧ M)It is not the case that both God and objective moral facts exist.¬G ∧ MGod does not exist, and objective moral facts do exist.

The first allows both to be false and allows either one to be true alone. It excludes only their conjunction. The second commits to one exact combination. Moving parentheses can change a worldview claim radically.

Another contrast is easy to miss. “Not A or B” might mean ¬A ∨ B, or it might mean ¬(A ∨ B). Natural language often under-specifies scope. Before symbolising, rewrite the sentence in unambiguous prose.

Retrieval check two

Let F mean “we are free” and D mean “determinism is true.” Which formula says that we are not both free and determined?

Conditionals have a direction

In A → B, A is the sufficient condition. A is enough for B. B is the necessary condition. A cannot hold without B.

PROSEIf an act is obligatory, then it is permissible.KEYO. The act is obligatory. P. The act is permissible.FORMO → P

Obligation is sufficient for permissibility. Permissibility is necessary for obligation. But permissibility is not sufficient for obligation. Helping a neighbour may be permissible without being required.

The words only if introduce a necessary condition. “A only if B” means A → B. By contrast, “A if B” means B → A. A useful test is to ask what the sentence rules out. “Salvation occurs only if grace is given” rules out salvation without grace, so S → G.

Retrieval check three

Let K mean “a person knows” and J mean “the person is justified.” Translate “Justification is necessary for knowledge.”

“If and only if” makes two promises

A biconditional combines both directions. A ↔ B is equivalent to (A → B) ∧ (B → A). Each claim is necessary and sufficient for the other.

Ordinary definitions often invite biconditionals. “An act is supererogatory if and only if it is morally good but beyond duty.” But beware of silently upgrading one-way claims. “Without consciousness there is no suffering” says that consciousness is necessary for suffering. It does not also say that every conscious being suffers.

A five-pass translation loop

  1. Atomise. List the smallest complete claims relevant to the inference.
  2. Key. Assign one stable letter to each atomic proposition.
  3. Connect. Translate not, and, or, if, and if-and-only-if.
  4. Group. Add parentheses to expose scope.
  5. Back-translate. Read the formula back into prose and check whether it still says what you meant.

Symbolisation does not settle whether a worldview claim is true. It makes the commitment precise enough to test.

Final dialogue exercise

“Objective morality exists only if God exists. But God exists if objective morality exists. Therefore, God exists if and only if objective morality exists.”

Without searching, send me a reply containing five moves.

  1. Define G and M.
  2. Translate each premise and the conclusion.
  3. Identify which condition is necessary and which is sufficient in each premise.
  4. Say whether the conclusion follows deductively.
  5. State one philosophical objection that challenges a premise rather than the inference.

I’ll check your symbols and then take the strongest opposing worldview position in a short mock debate. Ask me any follow-up question if a distinction remains unclear.
