Lesson 4 · Propositional logic · About 18 minutes

Make the structure visible

Propositional logic strips a sentence down to complete claims and the truth-functional connections between them. That loss of detail is deliberate: it lets you see exactly what must be true for the whole sentence to be true.

Lesson 3 distinguished arguments that promise necessity from arguments that offer probability or explanatory superiority. This lesson begins the machinery for testing the first kind. Symbolisation is useful because ordinary language carries emphasis, implication, and ambiguity alongside logical structure. A formula preserves less, but what it preserves is explicit. That makes it easier to spot a negation with the wrong scope, an inclusive “or” mistaken for an exclusive one, or a necessary condition quietly reversed into a sufficient condition.

Begin with complete claims. In truth-functional logic, or TFL, a sentence letter stands for a sentence that can be true or false. The textbook forall x: Calgary introduces this symbolisation key.

AIt is raining outside.CJenny is miserable.

The key does not assert either sentence. It records what the letters mean for this problem. Nor does the letter preserve the internal grammar of its sentence. Once “It is raining outside” is represented by A, TFL treats A as an atom: it can combine with other formulas, but TFL does not inspect “raining” or “outside.” This is why the key must use complete sentences rather than isolated subjects or predicates. A letter may stand for “Jenny is miserable,” but not merely for “Jenny” or “miserable.” The choice of letter is temporary and conventional; clarity comes from recording the choice and using it consistently.

Connect complete claims without losing their scope

Five connectives do the central work. Negation, ¬A, says it is not the case that A. Conjunction, A ∧ B, says both A and B. Disjunction, A ∨ B, says at least one of A and B. The conditional, A → B, says that if A then B. The biconditional, A ↔ B, combines both conditional directions, A → B and B → A. These are truth-functional connectives: once the truth values of their components are fixed, the truth value of the compound is fixed. They do not automatically preserve contrast, chronology, causation, explanation, probability, or obligation.

English words do not map mechanically to symbols. The textbook uses “Although Barbara is energetic, she is not athletic.” Let B mean “Barbara is athletic” and E mean “Barbara is energetic.” “Although” contributes a contrast in tone, but the truth-functional content says both that Barbara is energetic and that she is not athletic, giving E ∧ ¬B. The translation deliberately discards the contrast. If that contrast mattered to the argument, TFL would be too coarse a tool.

PROSEAlthough Barbara is energetic, she is not athletic.KEYB. Barbara is athletic. E. Barbara is energetic.FORME ∧ ¬B

Disjunction contains another trap. TFL uses inclusive “or”: A ∨ B is true when A alone is true, B alone is true, or both are true. The restaurant sentence “You get either soup or salad, but not both” is exclusive. Let S₁ mean “You will get soup” and S₂ mean “You will get salad.” The first part requires at least one option, S₁ ∨ S₂; the final clause excludes their conjunction, ¬(S₁ ∧ S₂). Putting those requirements together gives (S₁ ∨ S₂) ∧ ¬(S₁ ∧ S₂). This is a useful reminder that a single expression in English may require several formal connectives.

Example provenance. Reproduced unchanged apart from omitting outermost brackets from forall x: Calgary, sentence 24 and its analysis in §5.3, licensed CC BY 4.0.

The grouping marks are not decoration. Compare ¬(S₁ ∧ S₂), “It is not the case that you get both,” with ¬S₁ ∧ S₂, “You will not get soup but you will get salad.” The first excludes one combination—both items together—while allowing soup alone, salad alone, or neither. The second selects just one combination. If you are unsure about scope, identify the main connective: the final operation governing the entire formula. Then translate outward from it. In ¬(S₁ ∧ S₂), negation is main; in ¬S₁ ∧ S₂, conjunction is main.

Keep necessary and sufficient conditions facing the right way

Conditionals deserve extra care because English distributes “if,” “only if,” “necessary,” and “sufficient” around a sentence in ways that tempt reversal. In A → B, A is sufficient for B: having A is enough to have B. B is necessary for A: A cannot be true without B. This does not say that B is sufficient for A, and it does not say that A is necessary for B.

The textbook’s geographical pair makes the direction visible. Let P mean “Jean is in Paris” and F mean “Jean is in France.” “If Jean is in Paris, then Jean is in France” becomes P → F. Paris is sufficient for being in France, while being in France is necessary for being in Paris. But “Jean is in France only if Jean is in Paris” becomes F → P. That second claim is far stronger and, absent unusual travel arrangements, false: Jean could be in Lyon. The phrase following “only if” names the necessary condition.

P → FIf Jean is in Paris, then Jean is in France.F → PJean is in France only if Jean is in Paris.

A reliable procedure is to ask what combination the sentence forbids. “A only if B” forbids A without B, so its form is A → B. The same direction can be expressed by saying that B is necessary for A or that A is sufficient for B. Do not rely on where the words appear; translate the relationship. Also resist reading the arrow as “causes.” The formula says only that an A-true, B-false case is excluded. Whether A causes B, explains B, precedes B, or provides a reason for B lies outside the conditional’s truth-functional content.

Check the direction

Using D for “Laika is a dog” and M for “Laika is a mammal,” which formula symbolises “Laika is a dog only if she is a mammal”?

A biconditional states both directions. The textbook follows its first Laika sentence with “Laika is a dog if she is a mammal,” which has form M → D. Combining it with D → M produces (D → M) ∧ (M → D), abbreviated D ↔ M: “Laika is a dog if and only if she is a mammal.” The formula may be false, but its translation can still be accurate. Symbolisation first asks what was claimed; it does not improve the claim or certify its truth. In worldview arguments, that separation matters. If a speaker asserts that morality exists if and only if God exists, your first job is to expose both conditional commitments. Only then should you test whether either direction is defensible.

Combine the connectives

Let A mean “Alice is a spy,” B mean “Bob is a spy,” and C mean “The code has been broken.” Which formula symbolises “If either Alice or Bob is a spy, then the code has been broken”?

A compact translation routine is enough. First identify the smallest complete claims relevant to the inference and give them stable letters. Next paraphrase the sentence until its connective structure is explicit. Then construct the formula from the inside out, adding grouping marks wherever scope could be mistaken. Finally, read the result back into ordinary language. Back-translation catches many errors because it forces you to hear the claim your symbols actually make, especially around “only if” and negation.
