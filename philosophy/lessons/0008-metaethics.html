Lesson 8 · Metaethics · 10–15 minutes

What are we doing when we call something wrong?

Learn to separate the questions hidden inside moral language. You will be able to locate realism, error theory, expressivism, and relativism without forcing them onto one misleading line.

Your win for today

Given a metaethical claim, you will identify whether it concerns meaning, psychology, reality, objectivity, or knowledge—and avoid inferring an answer to one question from an answer to another.

Ethics beneath ethics

Normative ethics asks which acts are right, which character traits are virtues, or which outcomes matter. Metaethics asks what moral judgments mean, what kind of mental state they express, whether anything makes them true, and how we could know. “Lying is sometimes permissible” is a normative claim. “Moral truth depends on social standards” is a metaethical claim.

Metaethics is not morally neutral in the sense of being unimportant. It changes how you understand disagreement, evidence, motivation, and revision. Yet a metaethical view rarely hands you a complete decision procedure. Two realists can disagree about euthanasia. Two expressivists can disagree just as fiercely. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s overview of metaethics treats the field as a family of semantic, metaphysical, epistemological, and psychological questions.

Do not use one switch. Use four

MEANINGDo ordinary moral sentences purport to state propositions that can be true or false? MINDDoes sincerely judging something wrong centrally involve belief, desire-like disapproval, a plan, or some combination? WORLDAre any moral claims true, and what facts or properties—if any—make them true? AUTHORITYWould their truth hold independently of an individual’s or group’s actual attitudes and conventions?

These switches interact, but they are not identical. Even the labels are contested. Some philosophers build mind-independence into realism; others use a thinner idea of truth and objectivity. Treat every label as the beginning of clarification, not its end. The SEP entry on moral anti-realism explicitly warns that the realism vocabulary is not perfectly standardised.

When someone says “morality is subjective,” ask which claim they mean. A claim about language, mental states, truth-makers, or authority?

Cognitivism does not entail realism

Cognitivism says, roughly, that moral judgments express beliefs and moral sentences are apt for truth or falsity. Moral realists are normally cognitivists. They think at least some moral claims are true because the relevant moral facts obtain. Realists then divide. A naturalist may identify moral facts with natural facts; a non-naturalist may regard normativity as irreducible; other views develop truth, reasons, or construction in different ways. Realism is a neighbourhood, not one house. See the SEP survey of moral realism.

But a sentence can aim at truth and fail. Error theory combines cognitivism with a sweeping negative verdict. Ordinary positive moral claims purport to represent objective moral features, but no such features are instantiated, so those claims are systematically untrue. J. L. Mackie’s classic defence appeals to persistent variation in moral codes and to the alleged metaphysical and epistemological “queerness” of objective values. Crucially, Mackie says cultural variation by itself does not entail a second-order metaethical conclusion.

REALIST“Cruelty is wrong” asserts something truth-apt, and at least some claims of this kind are true. ERROR THEORISTIt asserts something truth-apt, but morality systematically fails to match the world as it purports to.

The disagreement is not about whether moral grammar looks assertoric. Both can accept that. It is about whether the world vindicates the assertion.

Expressivism is more than boo and hurrah

Noncognitivism denies that moral judgment is fundamentally ordinary fact-stating belief. Early emotivism was sometimes illustrated by treating “Stealing is wrong” as an expression of disapproval. Modern expressivists offer richer accounts. Moral judgments may express desire-like attitudes, commitments, norms, or plans. Saying an act is wrong can both reveal a practical stance and invite coordination around it. The SEP entry on cognitivism and noncognitivism traces emotivism, prescriptivism, norm-expressivism, and hybrid theories.

A serious expressivist must explain why moral claims behave logically. Consider, “If lying is wrong, getting your brother to lie is wrong.” The phrase “lying is wrong” appears inside a conditional; it is not simply being exclaimed. We can then reason from “Lying is wrong” to the conditional’s consequent. This embedding problem, often called the Frege–Geach problem, helped drive sophisticated expressivist semantics and quasi-realism. Quasi-realists try to earn the realist-seeming practices of moral truth, consistency, error, and disagreement from an expressivist starting point. Simon Blackburn’s Essays in Quasi-Realism is a central primary work.

Objectivism, relativism, and nearby views

Objectivism says moral status does not simply vary with the actual attitudes or conventions of each individual or group. Relativism makes the truth or justification of moral judgments relative to a framework, such as a culture’s standards. Thus one action might be right-relative-to-framework-A and wrong-relative-to-framework-B. This is stronger than descriptive relativism, the sociological observation that communities disagree. It is also different from tolerance. A relativist framework can contain intolerant norms, while an objectivist can defend tolerance as objectively required. The SEP entry on moral relativism distinguishes these theses and their motivations.

Subjectivism usually makes moral truth depend on individual attitudes. Constructivist theories instead ground or constitute moral truths through some actual or idealised practical procedure, standpoint, or rational construction. Whether a constructivist counts as realist depends on both the theory and the definition of realism. Likewise, a divine-command theory may be objective relative to human preferences while depending on a divine will. Calling it simply “mind-dependent” conceals which mind and what sort of dependence matter.

Theology does not settle metaethics. An atheist can be a robust moral realist and identify moral facts with natural facts or irreducible normative facts. A theist can ground morality in God, regard moral truths as necessary features of reality or divine nature, or adopt another account. “Without God, anything goes” and “religion makes morality subjective” are both compressed arguments with hidden premises, not definitions.

Diagnose disagreement before debating it

When two people disagree morally, move through four diagnostic questions.

  1. First-order claim. What act, policy, character, or outcome are they evaluating?
  2. Nonmoral facts. Do they disagree about consequences, intentions, risks, history, or who is affected?
  3. Normative standards. Do they weight welfare, rights, desert, loyalty, liberty, or virtue differently?
  4. Metaethical interpretation. Do they disagree about what moral claims do, what makes them true, or whose standards have authority?

Do not jump to step four. Persistent disagreement may support an argument against objectivity, but it can also reflect ignorance, bias, different nonmoral beliefs, or the difficulty of the subject. Conversely, agreement does not prove realism; shared attitudes may have shared evolutionary or cultural causes. Metaethics asks which explanation best survives comparison.

A moral dispute is data for metaethics, not an automatic verdict. Reconstruct the bridge from disagreement to worldview.

Final dialogue. Locate your own morality

Choose a moral claim you sincerely accept, preferably one that could guide a difficult decision. Send me five short answers.

  1. State the moral claim without using obvious, simply, or just.
  2. When you assert it, are you mainly describing a fact, expressing a practical stance, prescribing conduct, or doing several things?
  3. What, if anything, would make it true?
  4. Would it remain true if every human community rejected it? Explain why.
  5. Name evidence or an argument that could move your confidence.

I will classify each answer by question rather than force a single label on you. Then I will argue from the strongest incompatible position and test whether your answers form a coherent package. Ask me any follow-up question if one of these distinctions remains unclear.

Primary reading

Read chapter one, “The Subjectivity of Values,” in J. L. Mackie’s Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong. Reconstruct the argument from disagreement and the argument from queerness separately; Mackie himself cautions that observed variation does not entail his conclusion without further premises. For the opposing family of strategies, read the introduction to Simon Blackburn’s Essays in Quasi-Realism. Use the SEP overview to locate both within the wider field.

Mission link. A deliberate worldview needs more than moral confidence. It needs clarity about what moral claims are doing, what could make them true, and what disagreement can—and cannot—show.
