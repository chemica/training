Lesson 8 · Metaethics · About 20 minutes

What are you doing when you call something wrong?

Before asking which moral judgments are correct, metaethics asks what such judgments mean, what state of mind they express, and what—if anything—could make them true.

Lesson 7 compared ways of deciding what to do. Those were questions in normative ethics: which actions are right, which outcomes matter, and which traits are virtues? Metaethics changes level. When somebody says “Stealing is morally wrong,” are they describing a moral feature of stealing, expressing disapproval, prescribing conduct, or doing some combination of these? If the sentence is capable of truth, what would make it true? Would its truth depend on anyone’s attitudes? Could we know it?

These questions matter to a deliberate worldview because familiar slogans answer several of them at once without argument. “Morality is objective,” “morality is subjective,” and “without God anything goes” can each conceal claims about language, psychology, reality, knowledge, and authority. A useful map should separate those claims before attaching a school label. Labels in this area are genuinely unstable: the SEP notes that disagreement about the meaning of “realism” leaves “anti-realism” unsettled too.

Separate meaning from success

Cognitivism can be formulated at two related levels. As a claim about mental states, it treats moral judgments as beliefs; as a claim about speech acts, it says moral utterances express beliefs and function as assertions. Such beliefs and assertions purport to represent how things are and are candidates for truth and falsity. Noncognitivism denies the corresponding claim: moral judgments are not beliefs, or moral utterances do not express beliefs as assertions. These formulations should not be silently collapsed, even though philosophers often move between them. Either way, the cognitivist–noncognitivist divide concerns the kind of state or speech act involved; it does not yet tell us which moral claims are true.

An early noncognitivist picture, associated with A. J. Ayer, treats “Stealing money is wrong” as closer to voicing disapproval—roughly, saying “Stealing money!” in a condemning tone—than to attributing a property. This simple picture explains why moral language can express attitudes and engage motivation. It also risks losing features that moral language plainly seems to have. Modern expressivists therefore give more sophisticated accounts in terms of attitudes, plans, norms, or commitments rather than merely replacing ethical claims with boos and cheers.

A cognitivist may still deny every positive moral claim. Error theory says moral judgments aim at truth but systematically fail because the properties needed to make them true are absent or uninstantiated. The SEP compares this with an atheist’s interpretation of ordinary God-talk: “God exists” purports to state a fact, but an atheist denies that the world contains the entity required to make it true. Likewise, an error theorist can think “Stealing is morally wrong” is a genuine assertion while denying that anything instantiates objective wrongness.

A moral realist, in the robust sense used here, holds that moral facts or properties exist objectively and that at least some moral judgments succeed. Realists disagree about their nature. Naturalists try to place moral facts within the natural world; non-naturalists resist reducing normativity to natural properties. The crucial contrast with error theory is therefore not whether moral sentences look like assertions—both sides can agree that they do—but whether reality vindicates any of them.

Noncognitivism faces a different test. Moral language embeds inside larger sentences and participates in reasoning. Consider the conditional, “If stealing is morally wrong, then encouraging your brother to steal is morally wrong.” Here the antecedent is not simply an isolated outburst. Combine that conditional with “Stealing is morally wrong,” and the consequent follows by modus ponens. A theory of moral language must explain how the embedded words retain content suitable for the inference. This is the core pressure behind the Frege–Geach problem; sophisticated expressivists attempt to meet it rather than deny that moral reasoning exists.

Do not collapse objectivity, relativity, and disagreement

A further axis concerns dependence. Robust objectivism says moral facts do not obtain merely because an individual or group happens to approve of them. Non-objectivist theories allow moral facts but make them dependent, in some relevant sense, on attitudes, practices, institutions, or constructions. The phrase “mind-dependent” needs care. An action’s moral status might depend on intentions or suffering—both mental phenomena—without depending on whether observers approve of it. Always ask which mind, which attitude, and which kind of dependence is alleged.

The SEP’s diamond comparison makes the intended contrast vivid. That a diamond is carbon appears independent of what anyone thinks; everyone could deny it and be wrong. Its monetary value, however, depends on human practices and attitudes. The example does not prove that morality resembles either property. It shows what a non-objectivist is trying to claim: a fact may exist while depending on us.

Example provenance. Adapted from the diamond example in “Moral Anti-Realism,” §1. Changes: omitted the source’s illustrative price, compressed its counterfactual explanation, and added the warning that the analogy does not decide the moral case.

Relativism introduces another issue: whether the truth of a moral claim is relative to an individual or group. That is not simply the same as non-objectivism. The SEP offers a surprising combination. Someone might say different duties apply to different social castes and also say this hierarchy is an objective feature of the universe. Such a view would be relativist because duties vary by group, but objectivist about why they vary. Conversely, a society-dependent theory might say one absolute rule applies to everybody. The useful oppositions are therefore relative versus absolute, and objective versus non-objective; they need not line up.

Nor does observed disagreement settle any of these positions. Descriptive relativism reports substantial variation in moral judgments across societies. Metaethical relativism says, roughly, that moral truth or justification is relative to a framework. Moving from the first to the second requires an argument. Disagreement might arise from different factual beliefs, interests, histories, biases, or normative priorities; persistent disagreement may still be evidence relevant to metaethics, but it is not a deductive shortcut. Agreement would not prove realism either, because common judgments might share a cultural or evolutionary explanation.

Theism and atheism do not determine a metaethics by definition. A theist might ground obligations in divine commands, identify goodness with divine nature, or defend moral truths as necessary and not created by choice. An atheist might be an error theorist, expressivist, constructivist, naturalist realist, or non-naturalist realist. Even divine-command theory raises more than one axis: morality could be independent of human attitudes while depending on a divine attitude. The serious work begins after the slogan, when the dependence relation and its authority are made explicit.

Dialogue challenge

Choose one moral claim that genuinely guides you. State it without using “obviously” or “simply.” Then answer four questions. Does asserting it primarily describe, express, prescribe, or combine these functions? What would make it true, if it is truth-apt? Would it remain true if every human rejected it? What evidence or argument could alter your confidence? I will classify each answer by axis, identify any tensions, and defend the strongest incompatible package. Ask follow-up questions about any distinction that remains unclear.

Do not ask only whether morality is “real.” Ask what moral judgments express, whether they are truth-apt, whether any succeed, what makes them true, whose attitudes matter, and how we could know.
