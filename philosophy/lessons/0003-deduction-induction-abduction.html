What is this argument promising?

A good argument need not prove its conclusion. Your task is to identify whether an argument promises necessity, probability, or explanatory superiority, and then demand the kind of support it actually owes you.

Lessons one and two taught you to reconstruct an argument and to separate the truth of its premises from the validity of its inference. That test is indispensable when an argument is deductive. It becomes misleading, however, if it is used as the only test for every kind of reasoning. A weather forecast can be responsible without making rain logically inevitable; a diagnosis can be reasonable without ruling out every other disease. Calling either one “invalid” may be true in the technical deductive sense, yet miss the judgment that matters.

The useful first question is therefore not simply, “Is it valid?” Ask, “What strength and kind of support is this argument trying to provide?” Deduction aims to make a false conclusion impossible if its premises are true. Induction, in the sense used in this lesson, projects from observed cases or frequencies to an unobserved case or a wider pattern. Abduction recommends a hypothesis because of its explanatory relation to the evidence. Induction and abduction are both ampliative: their conclusions go beyond what their premises logically guarantee. The distinction between them is their basis. Induction appeals to a pattern in observations; abduction appeals to explanation. This is a practical three-way map, not a claim that every philosopher draws the boundaries identically.

See the different promises

Begin with deduction. The following argument is used in forall x: Calgary to introduce conditional elimination, also called modus ponens.

P1If Jane is smart, then she is fast. P2Jane is smart. CJane is fast.

Provenance. Reproduced unchanged apart from punctuation from forall x: Calgary, §17.4, “Conditional”, licensed CC BY 4.0.

The content tells us nothing about whether Jane really is smart or fast. The form nevertheless guarantees this: there is no possible assignment on which both premises are true and the conclusion false. That is the deductive promise. Judge the inference for validity; if it is valid, judge the premises for truth or acceptability. A valid argument with true premises earns the stronger verdict of soundness. Deduction is especially useful where you need to expose consequences already implicit in commitments: if someone grants both premises, denying the conclusion is no longer an available move.

Now compare an argument from the same open textbook.

P1Every winter so far, it has snowed in Calgary. CIt will snow in Calgary this coming winter.

Provenance. Reproduced unchanged from forall x: Calgary, §2.5, “Inductive arguments”, licensed CC BY 4.0.

The premise may be true while the coming winter is the first snowless one. The argument is therefore deductively invalid, but it is plainly intended as an induction, not as a watertight proof. Its conclusion is a prediction based on an observed regularity. The relevant questions are graded rather than binary. How many winters were observed? Were the observations reliable? Has anything relevant changed? Does background knowledge about Calgary’s climate make continuation more likely? Evidence can make an induction stronger or weaker without ever turning it into a deduction.

That last point prevents a common failure. “The conclusion could still be false” is a decisive objection to deductive validity, but not to induction. Fallibility is part of the inductive promise. A useful criticism must instead show that the evidence raises the conclusion’s probability too little: perhaps the sample is tiny, selected badly, measured unreliably, or drawn from circumstances unlike the case being predicted. The philosophical problem of induction goes deeper and asks what ultimately justifies projecting observed regularities at all. You need not settle that problem to distinguish a careful projection from a reckless one.

Abduction begins somewhere else. It starts with evidence in need of explanation and asks which hypothesis would make the best sense of it. Stanford’s survey offers a case in which Tim and Harry had a serious quarrel that ended their friendship, but are later seen jogging together. Their having reconciled would explain the new observation, so reconciliation becomes a hypothesis worth taking seriously.

ETim and Harry had a friendship-ending quarrel, but have now been seen jogging together. HThe best explanation presently considered is that they have reconciled. CThey have probably reconciled.

Provenance. Adapted from Igor Douven, “Abduction,” §1, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Changes: compressed the narrative into evidence–best-explanation–conclusion form, made the source’s claim that reconciliation is the best explanation explicit as a premise, added “presently considered” to mark the conclusion’s dependence on the available alternatives, and replaced “friends again” with “reconciled.”

This is not deduction. The evidence could be true while the conclusion is false. It is also not a simple frequency-based projection: no rate of post-quarrel reconciliation has been supplied. Its force comes from the comparative claim that reconciliation explains the observation better than the alternatives presently considered. Merely saying that reconciliation would explain the jogging would not be enough; many incompatible hypotheses can explain the same evidence. To assess the inference, ask whether reconciliation really explains the evidence, whether it fits what else is known, and whether a rival explains at least as well with fewer difficulties. “They remain estranged but have financial matters to discuss” might be such a rival. Stanford’s source adds that information to show how new evidence can remove the original hypothesis’s advantage. Abductive conclusions are therefore revisable: adding information cannot invalidate a genuine deductive consequence, but it can change which explanation is best.

Do not turn explanatory virtues into a mechanical points table. Simplicity, generality, and coherence with established theories can pull in different directions, and the virtues themselves remain philosophically disputed. Nor does “best of the explanations I considered” mean “probably true.” A poor shortlist can still have a winner. The disciplined abductive reasoner actively looks for serious rivals and asks what evidence would discriminate among them. This habit matters in worldview disputes. Theists and atheists may agree about an observation yet disagree over the candidate explanations, their background plausibility, or what counts as an explanatory cost. Stanford’s discussion presents both the theoretical virtues and the danger that the best candidate considered may merely be “the best of a bad lot”.

Choose the test the argument owes you

The argument’s wording will sometimes announce its aim with “must,” “probably,” or “best explanation,” but ordinary speakers are rarely so tidy. Interpret the argument charitably: choose the strongest kind of support that both fits the reasoning actually offered and makes the argument reasonable. Do not “improve” a weak prediction into a deductive proof the speaker never claimed, and do not rescue a failed deduction by quietly lowering its conclusion to a probability. Complex arguments can also combine modes. A scientist may abductively propose a theory, deductively derive a prediction from it, and inductively treat repeated successful tests as confirmation.
