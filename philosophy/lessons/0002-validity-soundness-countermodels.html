Truth is not validity

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to judge claims and inferences separately, then expose an invalid deductive inference by constructing one case in which its premises are true and its conclusion is false.

Lesson 1 separated an argument into premises and a conclusion. That makes two different investigations possible. You can ask whether its claims are actually true, and you can ask whether its premises succeed in guaranteeing its conclusion. Everyday disagreement often blurs these questions. Someone rejects a false conclusion and assumes the reasoning must therefore be bad; someone else notices a neat piece of reasoning and assumes its premises deserve belief. Logic becomes more useful when you refuse both shortcuts.

What follows from what?

Consider the following argument. Its wording is reproduced unchanged from forall x: Calgary, §2.1, a Creative Commons Attribution textbook.

P1Either the butler or the gardener did it. P2The butler didn’t do it. CThe gardener did it.

You do not need to know who actually committed the crime to assess the connection. Temporarily suppose both premises are true. The first leaves two candidates; the second eliminates the butler. Under those assumptions, denying the conclusion would leave nobody who could satisfy the first premise. The premises therefore guarantee the conclusion.

A deductive argument is valid when there is no case in which all its premises are true and its conclusion is false. Notice the conditional character of that definition. Validity does not say that the premises are true. It says what must be true if they are true together. Truth and falsity belong to individual claims; validity and invalidity belong to whole arguments.

Now compare a second argument, also reproduced unchanged from forall x: Calgary, §2.1.

P1If the driver did it, the maid didn’t do it. P2The maid didn’t do it. CThe driver did it.

The maid’s innocence does not identify the driver. Perhaps neither of them did it; a third person did. In that case the first premise is true, the second premise is true, and the conclusion is false. That single case is a counterexample, often called a countermodel once “case” has been made formally precise. It proves that the argument is invalid.

This gives you a practical test. First, grant every premise for the sake of argument. Next, deny the conclusion. Finally, ask whether you can describe a coherent case in which those assignments hold together. If you can, stop: you have proved invalidity. If your proposed case makes even one premise false, it has not tested the inference. Failure to find a countermodel is not automatically a proof of validity; your search may simply have been unimaginative. Later lessons will give you exhaustive truth tables and formal proofs for cases where intuition is not enough.

To refute deductive validity, preserve every premise and falsify the conclusion.

Validity can travel with falsehood

Validity alone cannot establish a conclusion because a valid argument may begin with false premises. The textbook makes the point with this deliberately absurd argument, reproduced unchanged from forall x: Calgary, §2.4.

P1Oranges are either fruit or musical instruments. P2Oranges are not fruit. COranges are musical instruments.

The second premise and conclusion are false, but the inference is valid. If both premises were true, the first would leave fruit and musical instrument as the alternatives, and the second would eliminate fruit. The silliness is doing useful work: it prevents factual plausibility from disguising the structure.

The reverse mistake is equally tempting. True claims do not make an inference valid merely by appearing beside one another. This argument is reproduced unchanged from forall x: Calgary, §2.4.

P1London is in England. P2Beijing is in China. CParis is in France.

All three claims are actually true, yet the premises do no work toward the conclusion. A case in which Paris is no longer in France could leave both premises untouched. The conclusion happens to be true; it does not follow from these premises. This is why collecting facts that agree with your preferred conclusion is not the same as arguing for it.

An argument is sound when it is valid and all its premises are actually true. Soundness is the combination needed for a deductive argument to establish its conclusion. This yields a disciplined order of work in debate. Test validity by granting the premises and looking for a countermodel; then, as a separate task, test each premise against evidence and objections. To rebut an argument, one successful route is enough: show that the inference is invalid or show that at least one premise is false. Do not claim to have done both when you have done only one.

Practise the two-pass discipline

Return to a deductive argument you genuinely care about—or an argument whose speaker presents its conclusion as guaranteed—perhaps concerning morality, religion, politics, or a technical decision. Reconstruct it before judging it. On the first pass, grant every premise and attempt to make the conclusion false. Write down a concrete countermodel if you find one. On the second pass, set validity aside and ask what justifies each premise. This order matters: objecting to a premise does not expose an invalid inference, and exposing an invalid inference does not show that a premise is false. Neither kind of objection, by itself, shows that the conclusion is false; it shows that this argument has not established it.
