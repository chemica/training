Lesson 1 · Argument reconstruction · 15–20 minutes

Make the reasoning visible

Learn to turn ordinary prose into explicit premises and a conclusion without weakening it, strengthening it beyond recognition, or quietly supplying what the speaker never committed to.

Your win for today

By the end, you will be able to reconstruct a short passage in standard form, distinguish stated claims from a proposed hidden premise, and explain why your reconstruction is fair.

Read for support, not sentence order

In this course, an argument is a collection of claims in which some claims, the premises, are offered as reasons for accepting another, the conclusion. That is a relationship between claims, not a feature of their position on the page. A conclusion can arrive first, last, or between its reasons. Words such as because, since, therefore, and so are useful clues, but they are not commands: the same words sometimes do other jobs. The reliable question is, “What is the writer trying to establish, and what are they offering in support?” This account paraphrases Southworth and Swoyer’s openly licensed discussion of how to identify the point of an argument and why sentence order is not decisive.

Standard form is simply a display convention: write each premise separately, then put the conclusion last. It makes the proposed support structure easy to inspect; it does not imply that the author originally spoke in that order. The convention and the exercise passages used below come from Southworth and Swoyer’s openly licensed Critical Reasoning: A User’s Manual, §2.4.

The source introduces standard form with a deliberately simple argument:

P1All Oklahomans are Sooners. P2Tom is an Oklahoman. CTom is a Sooner.

The three claims and their roles are reproduced unchanged; only the visual labels have been shortened to P1, P2, and C. Go directly to the original standard-form example.

The example isolates the display convention; it does not yet ask you to interpret messy prose or decide whether its premises are true. Those are separate jobs. When reconstructing ordinary language, the danger is tidying so aggressively that you replace the author’s reasoning with your own. Preserve uncertainty, strength, and scope: probably must not silently become certainly; some must not become all; and a suggestion must not become a factual claim.

Make hidden premises visible, but do not invent them

Speakers regularly omit a step they expect their audience to supply. Such an argument is often called an enthymeme. Recovering an omitted premise can expose the real point of disagreement, but it also creates an opportunity to smuggle your own beliefs into somebody else’s case.

Exercise 1 in the source provides a useful case:

Hillsborough County has issued a safer-at-home order. My neighbor Brian keeps going out in his shaved ice truck. So, he is violating the order.

The conclusion is explicit, but the stated facts do not yet tell us that driving the truck is something the order forbids. A cautious reconstruction can expose that missing connection without pretending to know the order’s complete contents:

P1Hillsborough County has issued a safer-at-home order. P2Brian keeps going out in his shaved ice truck. HPThe safer-at-home order prohibits going out in a shaved ice truck in the way Brian is doing. CBrian is violating the order.

The passage is reproduced unchanged; the proposed hidden premise and standard-form layout are additions made for this lesson. The source supplies no answer key. Go directly to the original exercise.

Notice what reconstruction does not settle. Labelling the hidden premise does not endorse it, establish that the order really has that scope, or prove the conclusion. It tells us which commitment would connect the stated facts to the conclusion. That is progress because the conversation can now focus on what the order prohibits and whether Brian’s conduct falls within that prohibition.

A useful discipline is to separate recovery from repair. Recovery makes explicit what the wording, context, and apparent intention give you reason to attribute to the speaker. Repair adds a premise that would make the argument better but that the speaker may never have accepted. When the evidence underdetermines the missing bridge, present it as a question: “Are you assuming that all Catholics should believe this?” That gives the speaker control over their argument. Charity means preferring a plausible interpretation among readings the text can bear; it does not mean donating whatever premise would rescue the inference.

A hidden premise should also do necessary work. If it merely repeats the conclusion, it explains nothing. If it introduces several new commitments when one would connect the stated reason to the conclusion, it obscures rather than clarifies. And if its antecedent requires a fact the stated premises never establish, the bridge still has a gap. The goal is the smallest clear commitment that captures the apparent route of support.

A fair four-pass method

  1. Locate. State the principal claim the passage is trying to support.
  2. List. Separate the stated reasons, retaining qualifications and removing only genuine repetition or non-argumentative material.
  3. Link. If the support has a gap, propose the smallest hidden premise licensed by wording, context, and apparent intention.
  4. Check. Read your version against the original. Ask whether the speaker could recognise it, whether every line earns its place, and whether you have marked additions as hidden rather than stated.

Do this before deciding whether you agree. Premature evaluation encourages motivated reconstruction: an opponent becomes conveniently foolish, while an ally receives generous repairs. A fair map can still reveal a bad argument. Its purpose is not to protect the speaker from criticism but to ensure that your criticism reaches the reasoning they actually offered.

Final dialogue exercise

Reconstruct this passage without looking at the earlier method:

The Iowa State Lottery has already contributed over 41 million dollars to Iowa’s massive economic development push. The funds have been used all around the state: business incentives, cultural program, agricultural research, trade and export development, education. When you play the lottery, all of Iowa wins.
  1. Write its principal conclusion as C.
  2. Write only its stated supporting claims as numbered premises.
  3. Identify whether the final sentence is literal, rhetorical, or ambiguous.
  4. Propose one minimal hidden premise, marking it HP.
  5. Name one change that would make your reconstruction less fair.
